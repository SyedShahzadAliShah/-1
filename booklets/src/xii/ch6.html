6.1 Entrepreneur and Entrepreneurship

An entrepreneur is a person who identifies a problem, thinks of a solution, and tries to change that solution into a product or service.

An entrepreneur does not always begin with a large company or a big office. Many begin with a small idea, limited resources, and a strong wish to solve a problem. Think of a young person in Karachi who starts by delivering home-cooked lunch boxes to a few offices, or a student in Sukkur who repairs mobile phones for neighbours. They started small.

Entrepreneurs usually show three habits:

  • They observe people and listen to their needs. They notice what annoys people again and again.
  • They take calculated risks. A calculated risk is a risk you take after thinking about what you could lose and how to keep that loss small. It is not gambling.
  • They improve their idea step by step. They do not expect the first attempt to be perfect.

What is entrepreneurship?

Entrepreneurship is the process of planning, starting, testing, and improving a business idea. It involves creativity, problem-solving, teamwork, communication, and calculated risk.

Notice the difference: the entrepreneur is the person; entrepreneurship is the process that person goes through.

Simple meaning. An entrepreneur asks: "What problem can I solve?" Entrepreneurship is the process of testing and improving that solution until it becomes useful for people.

Remember the three-step journey:

1. Problem→2. Solution→3. Product

Worked example: spotting the three steps. Ayesha lives in Larkana. Her mother and other women in the street make beautiful hand-embroidered rillis, but they can only sell them to visitors.

Step 1, Problem: skilled women have products but very few buyers.

Step 2, Solution: show the rillis online so buyers from other cities can see them.

Step 3, Product/Service: a Facebook page with photos, prices and a "message to order" button.

Ayesha is the entrepreneur. Planning the page, posting photos, seeing which designs sell, and changing prices is entrepreneurship.

Mistake: "Only rich people with big offices can be entrepreneurs."

Fix: The source clearly says many entrepreneurs begin with a small idea and limited resources. What matters is a real problem, a useful solution, and the willingness to improve it.

6.2 Entrepreneurship in the Digital Age ★ Golden

Entrepreneurship in the digital age means using digital tools to start, test, improve, and grow a business idea.

These tools may include mobile phones, social media, websites, online forms, digital payments, maps, videos, and simple apps. In Pakistan you already know many of them: WhatsApp, Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, Google Forms, Google Maps, and digital payments such as JazzCash, Easypaisa or Raast.

The power of digital tools

A digital entrepreneur does not always need to create a complex mobile app. Sometimes a WhatsApp group, a Google Form, a Facebook page, or a simple website is enough to test an idea.

In the past

  • Large investment needed.
  • Offices and shops had to be rented first.
  • Long planning before anyone saw the product.
  • A failed idea meant a big loss.

Today (digital age)

  • A small idea can be tested quickly.
  • Free tools: WhatsApp, Google Forms, Facebook.
  • Feedback arrives in hours or days.
  • If users do not like the idea, the team can change it before spending too much money.

Real-world relevance (from the source).

Example 1: A student selling handmade cards through Instagram. She posts photos of her Eid cards, people message her, and she delivers. No website, no app.

Example 2: A shopkeeper creating a WhatsApp catalogue for Ajrak. WhatsApp Business lets him list each Ajrak with a photo and price. Customers in Hyderabad or Karachi browse and order through chat.

Both are digital entrepreneurship without writing a single line of code.

Walk-through: how the Ajrak seller tests a new design cheaply.

  1. He photographs 3 new Ajrak designs and adds them to the catalogue (cost: almost zero).
  2. He sends the catalogue link to his customer broadcast list.
  3. After one week he counts the orders per design.
  4. He prints more of the design that sold most and stops the one nobody ordered.

He learned what customers want before printing a large stock.

Remember "S-T-I-G": digital tools help you Start, Test, Improve and Grow. Think: "Start The Idea Growing."

Mistake: "Digital entrepreneurship means building a mobile app."

Fix: An app is only one option. A WhatsApp group, Google Form, Facebook page or simple website is often enough, and much cheaper, to test whether people want your idea.

6.3 From Problem to Business Idea

A good business idea usually starts from observing daily life. Look around your home, college, market, village, or city and ask these simple questions:

  1. What problem do people face?
  2. Who faces this problem?
  3. How often does it happen?
  4. What do people currently do to solve it?
  5. Can technology help in a simple way?

A short way to remember them: What? Who? How often? Current fix? Tech help?

Example scenario (from the source): study material before exams.

Students cannot find study material before exams. The quick answer is "students are careless", but that is not the real issue.

Apply the five questions:

  1. What problem? Students cannot find notes before exams.
  2. Who? Students of a class or college.
  3. How often? Before every test and exam.
  4. Current fix? Notes are shared in many places: WhatsApp, email, photocopies, and class groups. Students search all of them.
  5. Tech help? Yes: a simple subject-wise notes organizer where all files are placed in one location (for example, one shared Google Drive folder per subject).

The real problem is scattered notes, and the idea solves exactly that.

Scope of the problem

Avoid choosing very large problems at the start, such as "improving the whole education system". A better problem is smaller and clear, such as helping Grade XII students track practical file submission dates. Smaller problems are easier to test and improve.

6.4 Prototype ★ Golden

A prototype is an early model of a product, service, website, or app. It shows how the idea may look or work. It is NOT the final product. It is made to test the idea and collect feedback.

Think of the clay model an architect makes before building a house, or the sample kameez a tailor stitches before making 50 for a shop. The model lets people say "make this bigger" or "move that here" before real money is spent.

Why is a prototype useful?

A prototype helps students and entrepreneurs explain their idea clearly, because users can see something instead of only listening to words.

  • It makes the idea visible.
  • It helps the team find mistakes early. Users can point out if a button is hard to find, if a screen has too much text, or if a feature is missing.
  • It saves time and money before final coding begins. Fixing a pencil sketch takes a minute; fixing finished code can take days.

What form can a prototype take?

A prototype may be a paper sketch, a cardboard model, a clickable PowerPoint, a Canva design, a Figma wireframe, a simple website layout, or a sample service process chart. A wireframe is a simple outline drawing of a screen that shows where things go (boxes, buttons, text) without colours or pictures.

For example, for a canteen pre-order system, students can just draw the screens on paper.

Worked example: how a paper prototype finds a mistake early. Bilal's team in a Nawabshah college draws the three screens above on A4 paper.

  1. They hand screen 1 to a classmate and say, "Order a samosa."
  2. The classmate looks for a quantity box and cannot find one. She asks, "What if I want two?"
  3. The team notes: feature missing: quantity.
  4. They add a small "Qty: [ ]" box with a pencil in 30 seconds.

If they had already coded an app, adding quantity would have meant changing the database, the screen and the order list. The prototype saved time and money.

Prototype = "practice model". It is like a rough draft of an essay: you show it, get corrections, and rewrite it before the final copy.

Mistake: Thinking a prototype must work or must look beautiful.

Fix: A prototype only has to make the idea visible so users can react. A pencil sketch is a valid prototype.

6.5 Types of Prototypes

Prototypes are commonly divided into three types according to their level of detail, called fidelity. Fidelity means how close the prototype is to the final product. Low fidelity = far from final; high fidelity = very close to final.

6.5.1 Low-Fidelity Prototype

A quick and rough model made at the very beginning. It is drawn on paper. It does not focus on colours or fonts. It shows the main idea and flow (which screen comes after which). It is very suitable for students because it is cheap, fast, and easy to change.

6.5.2 Mid-Fidelity Prototype

Clearer than a paper sketch. It is created in PowerPoint, Canva, or Figma. It shows screens and navigation in a more organised way, but without final graphics. It is useful to test screen-to-screen navigation, for example by linking slides so that clicking "Next" opens the next screen.

6.5.3 High-Fidelity Prototype

Looks very close to the final product. It includes realistic colours, buttons, sample data, and working features. It takes more time and should only be created after the basic idea is tested.

Comparison of prototype types

For most school and college projects, you should begin with a low-fidelity prototype. A common mistake is to start building a full app before knowing whether users need it. A rough prototype protects you from this mistake.

6.6 Prototype Development Cycle ★ Golden

Prototype development is not a one-time activity. A prototype is created, tested, improved, and tested again. This repeating process is called iteration. The main stages are Design, Build, Test, and Iterate.

StageWhat happens
1. DesignUnderstand the problem and plan the solution. Decide what the prototype should show (e.g. screen flow, features).
2. BuildCreate the first version (physical or digital). The aim is not beauty; the aim is to make the idea visible.
3. TestShow the prototype to users. Observe what they understand and what they find difficult.
4. IterateImprove the prototype based on user feedback. Remove unnecessary parts and add missing features.

Worked example: two rounds of the cycle. A team in Thatta wants a WhatsApp catalogue for a local Ajrak shop.

Round 1

  • Design: The problem is that customers do not know which designs and prices are available. Plan: one page per design with photo, price and an "Order" message.
  • Build: They make a paper booklet with 6 hand-drawn "catalogue pages".
  • Test: They show it to 5 customers. Three ask, "Is this single or double-sided Ajrak? What size?"
  • Iterate: They add size and type to every page.

Round 2

  • Design: Add the size and type fields, and a delivery note.
  • Build: They make the catalogue in WhatsApp Business with real photos.
  • Test: 5 new customers browse it. Now nobody asks about size; one says the delivery charge is unclear.
  • Iterate: They add "Delivery in Thatta: Rs 100" to each item.

Each round produces a clearer version, because each change comes from real feedback.

Mnemonic: "Do Big Things Iteratively" = Design, Build, Test, Iterate. And remember the arrow at the end goes back to Design: it is a cycle, not a line.

Mistake 1: Drawing the stages as a straight line that ends at "Iterate". Fix: Always draw a loop with an arrow from Iterate back to Design.

Mistake 2: Making the Build stage beautiful. Fix: The source says "The aim is not beauty; the aim is to make the idea visible."

6.8 Minimum Viable Product (MVP) ★ Golden

A Minimum Viable Product (MVP) is the simplest working version of a product. It includes only the most important features needed to test the idea with real users.

  • Minimum: it has only the essential features. No extras.
  • Viable: it is usable enough to actually solve the main problem. ("Viable" means able to work successfully.)

An MVP is not the final product. It is also not only a drawing. It is a small working version used to test whether users actually need the solution.

Example from the source: canteen app.

Complete canteen app (final product)Canteen MVP
Login, online payment, delivery tracking, discount offers…A simple Google Form where students select snacks before break.

Step-by-step: how to cut a big idea down to an MVP.

  1. List all features you dream of: login, payment, delivery tracking, discounts.
  2. Ask of each one: "Without this, can a student still pre-order a snack?"
  3. Login? Not needed; the student can type name and class. Payment? Not needed; pay at the counter. Delivery tracking? Not needed; students collect. Discounts? Not needed.
  4. What is left is the core: choose snack + name + class. A Google Form does this for free.

That form is minimum (no extras) and viable (students really can pre-order with it).

Simple meaning. An MVP answers: "Will people actually use this solution?" It tests real use and behaviour, not only the visual design.

Analogy: before opening a full restaurant, a cook in Hyderabad sells biryani from home to office workers for two weeks. If people keep ordering, the idea is worth growing. That home kitchen is the MVP.

Mistake 1: Calling a paper sketch an MVP. Fix: A sketch is a prototype. An MVP must work and be used by real users.

Mistake 2: Thinking MVP means a low-quality or broken product. Fix: It is small but viable; it must genuinely solve the main problem.

6.9 Difference between Prototype and MVP

Prototype and MVP are related, but not the same. A prototype is mainly used to show and test the design of an idea. An MVP is used to test real use and market demand. Market demand means whether enough people want the product and will use (or pay for) it.

Usually a prototype comes first and an MVP comes after it: Problem → Idea → Prototype → Feedback → MVP.

PointPrototypeMVP

Key takeaway

Prototype = "Does it look right and make sense?"

MVP = "Will people actually use it and find value in it?"

Memory hook: Prototype → Picture of the idea; MVP → Market test of the idea.

Worked example: classify each item. Decide whether each is a prototype or an MVP and give the reason.

  2. A WhatsApp broadcast group that really sends van arrival times to parents of one route for a week. → MVP: it works and real users use it daily.
  3. Clickable PowerPoint screens of a notes organizer. → Prototype (mid-fidelity): links work, but nobody uses it for real notes.
  4. A shared Google Drive folder per subject that a class actually uses before the monthly test. → MVP: real use in daily life.

Rule used: Does it work, and is it used by real users in real life? If yes → MVP. If it only shows the design → prototype.

Mistake: Thinking "high-fidelity prototype" and "MVP" are the same because both look good.

Fix: The difference is purpose and real use, not appearance. A beautiful Figma design that nobody uses for real orders is still a prototype; a plain Google Form taking real orders is an MVP.

6.10 Identifying the Riskiest Assumption

Every business idea is based on assumptions. An assumption is something we believe to be true, but have not tested yet.

The riskiest assumption is the belief that can make the entire business fail if it turns out to be wrong.

Entrepreneurs should identify and test the riskiest assumption early, because it saves time and money. If it is wrong, you find out before building everything else.

Table 6.7: Testing risky assumptions

Business IdeaPossible Risky AssumptionHow to Test It (MVP)
Online tuition finderStudents will pay for online tutoring.Ask 20 students and offer a trial class to see if they sign up.
Canteen pre-order formStudents will order snacks before break.Test a Google Form for one week in real time.
Ajrak online catalogueCustomers will place orders through WhatsApp.Share catalogue with 30 customers and record actual orders.
College van update systemParents and students will check van updates daily.Create a trial WhatsApp broadcast group for one route.

Notice that every test measures what people actually do (sign up, order, check), not just what they say.

Worked example: finding the riskiest assumption. Idea: a home-tutor booking service for Grade IX–X students in Sukkur.

Step 1, list assumptions:

  1. Parents want home tutors for their children.
  2. Good tutors in Sukkur are willing to join.
  3. Parents will pay a booking fee through our service instead of finding tutors through relatives.
  4. Parents will like our logo.

Step 2, ask of each: "If this is wrong, does the whole business fail?" Logo (4): no, easily changed. Parents wanting tutors (1): very likely true; many already hire tutors. Tutors joining (2): important, but easy to check by phoning a few tutors. Paying a booking fee (3): if parents refuse to pay, the business earns nothing, so this is the riskiest assumption.

Step 3, design a small MVP test: post the offer in 3 parent WhatsApp groups for one week, offer a first booking at a small fee, and count how many actually pay.

Ask: "What would kill this idea?" The answer is usually the riskiest assumption. Test it first, cheaply, with real behaviour.

Mistake: Testing easy, safe assumptions (colours, names) first and leaving the dangerous one for later.

Fix: Test the assumption that could make the whole business fail first. Also, do not trust only "Yes, I would use it". Measure actual sign-ups or orders.

6.13 Testing an MVP

MVP testing means giving the basic working version to real users and studying their response. The team should collect both numbers and comments.

  • Numbers (quantitative data) show how many users tried the MVP and how many may use it again.
  • Comments (qualitative data) explain what users liked, disliked, or found confusing.

Testing should be planned before the MVP is launched. Decide:

  1. How many users will test it.
  2. How long the test will continue.
  3. What information will be collected.

Table 6.10: Feedback during MVP testing

Type of FeedbackQuestionExample Result
Number-basedHow many users tried it?40 students used the form.
Number-basedHow many would use it again?30 out of 40 said yes.
Comment-basedWhat was confusing?Did not know order closing time.
ObservationWhere did users face difficulty?Forgot to enter class name.

Observation means the team watches users and notes difficulties themselves, even ones users do not mention.

Worked example: studying the results in Table 6.10.

  1. Repeat-use rate = (users who would use it again ÷ users who tried it) × 100 = (30 ÷ 40) × 100 = 75%.
  2. Did not want to use again = 40 − 30 = 10 students = (10 ÷ 40) × 100 = 25%.
  3. Comment: "Did not know order closing time" → Fix: write "Orders close 20 minutes before break" at the top of the form and in the WhatsApp message.
  4. Observation: "Forgot to enter class name" → Fix: make the Class field required in the Google Form so it cannot be submitted empty.
  5. Decide what to fix first: the closing-time problem stops people from ordering at all, so fix it first; the class-name problem only makes collection slower.

Three out of four users would come back, which is a promising sign; fixing the two problems could raise that number further.

Data skill link. Feedback is useful only when it is studied: Count the responses → Look for repeated problems → Decide which improvement comes first. Remember "C-L-D: Count, Look, Decide."

Mistake: Launching the MVP first and deciding later what to measure.

Fix: Plan the number of users, the test length and the questions before launch. Otherwise you may forget to record important data (for example, how many people opened the form but did not submit).

6.14 Beachhead Market ★ Golden

A beachhead market is a small and focused group of first users. These users have a clear need and are likely to try the product early.

Instead of trying to serve everyone, entrepreneurs first focus on one small group. This helps them test the MVP properly and improve it before expanding.

The word comes from the army: a beachhead is the small piece of beach soldiers capture first when they land; from there they move inland. In business, your beachhead is the first small group you "win".

Characteristics of a good beachhead market

  • It should be specific and narrowly defined.
  • It should be reachable by the startup team.
  • The users should have a strong, immediate need for the solution.

For example, targeting "all students in Pakistan" is too broad for a new study app. "Grade XII students preparing for board exams in one college" is much more focused and easier to reach.

Choosing a small first market does not mean the idea will remain small forever. After learning from this group, the idea can be improved and expanded, even globally.

Fix: A broad market is hard to reach and hard to learn from. Win a small group first, learn, improve, then expand.

6.16 Ethical and Safe Use of Digital Tools

Digital entrepreneurship should be responsible. When collecting user feedback, you must respect:

  • Privacy: people's personal information belongs to them.
  • Truthfulness: report results honestly; do not make up numbers or hide bad feedback.
  • Fairness: treat all users equally.

You should not collect unnecessary personal information or share phone numbers or photos without permission.

Equity point

A good digital solution should consider users who have weak internet, limited devices, or language difficulty. A design with large buttons, simple Urdu/Sindhi instructions, and low data usage helps ensure equal access. Equity means making sure everyone, including people with fewer resources, can use the solution.

Worked example: fix an unethical feedback form. A team's canteen feedback form asks:

  1. Full name, father's name, CNIC/B-form number, home address. → Problem: unnecessary personal data. Fix: ask only class (or nothing personal at all).
  2. "Upload your selfie." → Problem: photos are not needed and could be misused. Fix: remove.
  3. The team plans to post all respondents' phone numbers in the class group. → Problem: sharing numbers without permission. Fix: never share; keep responses private to the team.
  4. The form is in English only and has a 5 MB video at the top. → Problem: excludes users with language difficulty or weak internet. Fix: add Urdu/Sindhi instructions and remove the heavy video.

When presenting, the team reports "30 out of 40 would use it again" exactly, without rounding it up to "almost everyone". That is truthfulness.

Ask yourself "P-T-F-E": Is it Private, Truthful, Fair, and Equal-access?

Mistake: "More data is always better, so let's collect everything."

Fix: Collect only what you need to answer your test questions. Extra personal data creates risk for users and gives you no benefit.

Chapter 6 Review

Chapter summary

  • Entrepreneur: a person who identifies a problem, thinks of a solution, and turns it into a product or service; observes people, takes calculated risks, improves step by step.
  • Entrepreneurship: the process of planning, starting, testing and improving a business idea until it becomes useful (Problem → Solution → Product).
  • Digital age: entrepreneurs use accessible digital tools (social media, forms, websites, WhatsApp, digital payments) to start and test ideas quickly with low investment; often no coding is needed.
  • A digital idea must solve a real problem for real users, not just sound modern (Table 6.1 examples: notices, embroidery shop, test dates, canteen queues).
  • Business ideas come from observing daily life and talking directly to users: Observe → Ask Users → Select One Problem → Suggest Solution → Test Early. Start with a small, clear problem.
  • Prototype: an early model used to show and test the design of an idea; not the final product; makes the idea visible, finds mistakes early, saves time and money.
  • Fidelity is how close a prototype is to the final product: Low (paper), Mid (PowerPoint/Canva/Figma, tests navigation), High (realistic, Figma/HTML, only after the idea is tested).
  • Development cycle: Design → Build → Test → Iterate, repeated (iteration). Feedback is crucial; a prototype fails only if useful feedback is ignored.
  • MVP: the simplest working version with only essential features (Minimum) that still solves the main problem (Viable); it tests real use and market demand.
  • Prototype vs MVP: prototype asks "Does it look right?"; MVP asks "Will people actually use it?" A prototype may not work; an MVP must.
  • Riskiest assumption: the untested belief that, if wrong, will make the business fail; test it early with real behaviour.
  • MVP plan: problem, target users, ONE main feature, basic design, cost, marketing, feedback, improvement plan. Do not build everything at once.
  • Canteen case study: paper prototype → Google Form MVP with 5 items for 3 days → staff found too many items hard → kept 5 items, separate line, orders close 20 minutes before break.
  • MVP testing: plan users, duration and data in advance; collect numbers (40 tried, 30 would return = 75%) and comments/observations; count, look for repeated problems, fix the most important first.
  • Beachhead market: a small, focused, reachable group of first users with a strong, immediate need; win it first, then expand.
  • Ethics and equity: respect privacy, truthfulness and fairness; collect no unnecessary data; share nothing without permission; design for weak internet, limited devices and Urdu/Sindhi speakers.

Short questions — model answers

  1. An entrepreneur is a person who identifies a problem, thinks of a solution and tries to change that solution into a product or service. Entrepreneurship is the process of planning, starting, testing and improving a business idea, involving creativity, problem-solving, teamwork, communication and calculated risk. The entrepreneur is the person; entrepreneurship is the process.
  2. It means using digital tools such as mobile phones, social media, websites, online forms, digital payments, maps, videos and simple apps to start, test, improve and grow a business idea. Examples: a student selling handmade cards through Instagram; a shopkeeper making a WhatsApp catalogue for Ajrak.
  3. What problem do people face? Who faces this problem? How often does it happen? What do people currently do to solve it? Can technology help in a simple way?
  4. Small, clear problems are easier to test and improve. Too large: "improving the whole education system". Suitable: "helping Grade XII students track practical file submission dates".
  5. A prototype is an early model of a product, service, website or app that shows how the idea may look or work; it is not the final product and is made to test the idea and collect feedback. Useful because it makes the idea visible, helps find mistakes early (hard-to-find buttons, too much text, missing features), and saves time and money before final coding.
  6. Low-fidelity: quick, rough paper model showing main idea and flow, e.g. hand-drawn homework-reminder screens. Mid-fidelity: clearer model in PowerPoint/Canva/Figma showing layout and navigation without final graphics, e.g. clickable PowerPoint screens. High-fidelity: detailed model close to the final product with realistic colours, buttons, sample data and working features, e.g. a working demo of a small order-form website.
  7. An MVP (Minimum Viable Product) is the simplest working version of a product with only the most important features, used to test the idea with real users. Minimum: only the essential features, no extras. Viable: usable enough to actually solve the main problem.

Long questions — model answers

  1. Prototype development cycle. Draw four boxes in a loop: Design → Build → Test → Iterate → back to Design. Prototype development is not one-time; repeating the cycle is called iteration.
    • Design: understand the problem and plan what the prototype shows. ClassBuddy: interview 10 students; 7 of 10 forget tasks, so show a daily task list; reminders are needed before leaving home, so add a morning notification; students want simplicity, so use large buttons and clear labels.
    • Build: create the first version to make the idea visible (not beautiful). ClassBuddy: three paper screens (Today's classes, Add homework, Show reminder) joined by arrows.
    • Test: show it to users and observe. ClassBuddy: five classmates try "add one homework task" while the observer watches silently.
    • Iterate: improve from feedback. ClassBuddy: larger, centred Add Homework button; subject name and due date fields; a Weekly View button.
    Negative feedback is information that shows what to fix; the prototype fails only if the team ignores useful feedback.
  2. Prototype vs MVP. Purpose: prototype shows how the idea may look or work; MVP tests whether users will actually use it. Working status: prototype may not work (sketch or model); MVP must work at least in a basic way. Users: prototype, a small group giving design feedback; MVP, early real users in daily life. Cost: prototype very low; MVP low but may need basic development. Example: prototype = paper screens for a canteen order system; MVP = Google Form used for real canteen orders for three days. Key questions: prototype, "Does it look right and make sense?"; MVP, "Will people actually use it and find value in it?"
  3. MVP plan: college van update system (Hyderabad).
    • Problem: students and parents do not know when the van will arrive, so students wait outside for a long time or miss the van.
    • Target users: students and parents on one van route.
    • Main feature: the driver or a route monitor sends a short "Van left / 5 minutes away" message.
    • Basic design: one WhatsApp broadcast message with route stop name, time and status; voice note option for parents who cannot read.
    • Cost: free, using a WhatsApp broadcast list on the monitor's phone.
    • Marketing: a message in the class WhatsApp group and a printed slip for parents with the number to save.
    • Feedback: after one week, a 3-question Google Form or phone call: Did you check the updates daily? Did you wait less? Would you continue?
    • Improvement plan: if messages arrive too late, send them earlier; if many parents do not read, switch to voice notes; then expand to a second route.
    • Riskiest assumption: parents and students will check van updates daily. Test: trial broadcast group for one route for one week, counting how many people read or reply to messages each day.
    • Beachhead market: students and parents of ONE specific college van route.
  4. MVP plan: topi and Ajrak shop in Hala.
    • Low-fidelity prototype: paper catalogue pages, one per product, with a sketch, price, size/type and "Message to order"; shown to 5 customers to see if information is clear.
    • MVP: a WhatsApp Business catalogue with real photos and prices for 10–15 best-selling items; orders by chat; payment by cash on delivery or JazzCash/Easypaisa.
    • Riskiest assumption: customers will actually place orders through WhatsApp. Test: share the catalogue with 30 existing customers and record actual orders for two weeks.
    • Beachhead market: existing customers of this one shop (people who have bought before and whose numbers the shop already has with permission).
    • Marketing: broadcast message to existing customers, a QR code at the shop counter, posts in local Facebook groups.
    • Ethics: share no customer numbers or photos without permission; give truthful prices, sizes and photos. Equity: simple Urdu/Sindhi descriptions and compressed photos for low data use.
    • Improvement plan: drop designs nobody orders, add more of the popular ones, then expand to customers in Hyderabad and Karachi.
  5. Lost and Found MVP analysis.
    • (a) Tried: (45 ÷ 60) × 100 = 75% of the class. Repeat-use rate: (36 ÷ 45) × 100 = 80%. Confused about collection: (12 ÷ 45) × 100 = 26.7% (about 27%).
    • (b) "45 tried" and "36 would use again": number-based. "Did not know where to collect": comment-based (12 users said it). "Many reports had no photo": observation.
    • (c) Fix the collection confusion first: it is a repeated problem (more than a quarter of users) and it stops the system's main purpose, which is returning items to owners. Add "Collect found items from the library desk" at the top of the form and pin it in the WhatsApp group. Next, make the photo field strongly encouraged (or required for found items), since photos help owners recognise items. Re-test for another week and compare the numbers.

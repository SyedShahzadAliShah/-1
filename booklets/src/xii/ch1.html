1.1 Fundamentals of Human-Computer Interaction

Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) is the study and design of technology so that it serves people. An HCI system is easy to use, efficient, and responsive to human needs. HCI is also known as CHI (Computer-Human Interface) or MMI (Man-Machine Interaction). The three names mean the same idea: the meeting point of a person and a machine.

HCI is not only "making a pretty screen". It asks: Can a student in Hyderabad find results on the school portal in 30 seconds? Can a grandmother send JazzCash without help? Can a person standing at an outdoor ATM in bright sunlight read the buttons?

Sensory channels of interaction

Humans and computers communicate through sensory channels. A sensory channel is a path that carries information to (or from) one of the human senses. Computers must send and receive signals on the same channels, or the conversation breaks.

ChannelHow humans use itHow computers respond
Sight (بصارت)Look at screens, graphics or videos.Displays visuals on monitors and screens.
Touch (چھونا)Tap, type or swipe.Responds with clicks or vibrations (haptics).
Hearing (سننا)Listen to sounds or voices.Provides audio feedback via speakers.
Voice (آواز)Speak commands (for example, "Alexa").Processes speech and replies.
Spatial (مکانی)Move in physical space.Tracks position or body movement (for example, VR).

Haptics means physical feedback you can feel, such as a phone vibrating when a JazzCash payment succeeds. Spatial interaction means the system notices where your body is, as in a VR headset or a Kinect camera.

Worked example: map a JazzCash payment to channels. Sara in Sukkur sends Rs 500 to her brother.

  1. Sight: she looks at the JazzCash home screen and the amount field.
  2. Touch: she taps "Send Money" and types the mobile number.
  3. Voice (optional): she could say "JazzCash, send five hundred rupees" if voice commands are on.
  4. Hearing + haptics: a beep and a short vibration confirm "Payment successful".

If the app showed no tick, no sound and no vibration, Sara would not know whether the money left. That is an HCI failure, not a banking failure.

Remember the three names: HCI = CHI = MMI. Think "Human meets Machine." Five channels: S-T-H-V-S — Sight, Touch, Hearing, Voice, Spatial. "See The Handy Voice in Space."

Mistake: Writing "HCI is only about computer hardware" or mixing up Voice with Hearing.

Fix: HCI is about the design of the conversation between people and machines. Hearing is what you listen to (speakers). Voice is what you speak (microphone + speech software).

1.1.1 Traditional Interaction and 1.1.2 Natural Interaction

1.1.1 Traditional interaction is the basic way people use everyday devices. It relies on standard input and output: a mouse, a keyboard, a keypad, a monitor, speakers, or a simple touchscreen tap and swipe.

  • Computer: moving a mouse and typing on a keyboard; output via monitor and speakers.
  • Smartphone (traditional use): tapping and swiping on a touchscreen; output via screen lighting and vibrations.

1.1.2 Natural interaction is advanced, smart technology that lets people interact without a mouse or keyboard. It feels more human — you speak, move, or show your face, and the system understands.

  • Voice: speaking commands into a device (for example, "Alexa, play a song").
  • Body and gesture: facial recognition to unlock a phone, or playing VR games by waving hands.
FeatureTraditionalNatural
Main inputMouse, keyboard, keypad, simple tap/swipeVoice, face, hand/body gesture, movement
Feels likeOperating a machineTalking or moving as you do with people
ExamplesTV remote, ATM keypad, school-portal login typed on a PCFaceID, Siri, Kinect games, "Hey Google"
Typical outputScreen, speakers, vibrationSpoken reply, unlock, tracking of the body in VR

Worked example: sort today's tools. List three traditional and three natural interfaces you might use in one day in Sindh.

Traditional: (1) HBL ATM keypad to withdraw cash. (2) College computer lab: mouse + keyboard to open the school portal. (3) Television remote at home.

Natural: (1) Unlocking a phone with FaceID. (2) Asking Google Assistant "Call Aslam". (3) A VR science demo in which you pick a molecule by waving your hand.

Notice: the same smartphone can be traditional (you type a PIN) or natural (the camera reads your face). The classification depends on how you interact, not on the brand of the device.

Test sentence: If you must press keys or tap labelled buttons, it is traditional. If the machine understands speech, face or body movement, it is natural.

Mistake: "Touchscreens are always natural interaction."

Fix: Ordinary tapping and swiping on a phone is still listed as traditional in this chapter. Natural interaction starts when the system understands voice, face, or full-body gesture without a mouse or keyboard.

1.2 Applications of HCI (Domains of Life) ★ Golden

HCI is used to design and develop interactive systems such as desktop applications, websites and mobile apps. These are human-centred solutions built to meet user needs across many domains of life. A domain is an area of daily activity — health, money, school, talking to people.

When you design for a domain, you design for that domain's users, tasks and risks. A mistake on a games app is annoying. A mistake on a medical or banking screen can harm a person or lose money. That is why examiners treat this topic as golden.

DomainApplication examplesUser benefit
Health carePatient portals, telemedicine apps, assistive technologiesImproves access to medical services remotely
BankingMobile banking apps, secure ATM interfacesEnables quick transactions and secure financial management
EducationE-learning platforms (LMS), online exam portalsEnhances teaching, learning and student engagement
Networking and communicationSocial media, messaging apps (WhatsApp), ZoomConnects people globally through collaboration tools

Worked example: the same HCI idea in three Pakistani systems.

Step 1 — ATM (banking). You insert a card, the screen shows large buttons (Fast Cash, Balance, Other), you enter a PIN on the keypad, and you take cash. HCI benefit: a quick, secure transaction without a human teller.

Step 2 — JazzCash (banking + communication). You log in with a PIN or fingerprint, choose Send Money, type a number and amount, confirm, and see "Successful" with a JazzCash SMS. HCI benefit: money moves in seconds from a shop in Larkana to a student in Karachi.

Step 3 — School portal (education). A Grade XII student opens the college LMS, taps "Download date sheet", and submits a leave form. HCI benefit: learning and admin tasks happen without standing in a queue at the office.

Step 4 — Compare the user benefit. Banking HCI saves time and keeps money safe. Education HCI raises engagement. Health HCI (a Sehat/telemedicine app) brings a doctor to a village phone. Networking HCI (WhatsApp, Zoom) keeps a family in Dubai in touch with parents in Sukkur.

Exam mnemonic H-BEN: Health, Banking, Education, Networking. For each, write: one application + one user benefit. That structure scores full marks on "applications of HCI".

Mistake: Listing brand names only ("WhatsApp, JazzCash, Zoom") without saying the domain and the user benefit.

Fix: Always write three parts: domain → example → what the user gains. "Banking: JazzCash mobile app — enables quick, secure transfers."

1.3 Components of HCI

HCI relies on several primary components that work together. If one component is weak, the whole interaction feels broken. The first four are the people, the machine, the conversation, and the job to be done.

ComponentMeaning
iUser (صارف)Any individual or group that interacts with a computer system to achieve defined goals. Two categories: end users (people who directly operate the system, from beginners to experts) and stakeholders (people with an interest in the outcome, such as managers and clients).
iiComputer system (کمپیوٹر سسٹم)The platform where humans and computers communicate through input, output and feedback using an interface. It connects human actions with machine responses.
iiiUser interaction (صارف کا تعامل)The process through which a user communicates with a system by performing actions (clicking, tapping, typing, speaking). It transforms user intent into system responses.
ivTask (کام)The specific action users perform (for example, booking a ticket). It has three characteristics: task complexity (filling a form vs designing a 3D model), user knowledge (basic typing vs advanced programming), and time requirement (ATM withdrawal vs online passport application).

Worked example: label the four components of an HBL ATM withdrawal.

  1. User: end user = the account holder standing at the machine; stakeholder = the bank that owns the ATM and must keep it safe and working.
  2. Computer system: the ATM hardware plus the bank's software; it takes PIN input, shows a screen, and dispenses notes (output and feedback).
  3. User interaction: insert card → type PIN → choose "Cash" → type amount → confirm. Intent ("I want Rs 5,000") becomes a machine response (cash + receipt).
  4. Task: withdraw cash. Complexity: low (a few buttons). User knowledge: basic (PIN and amounts). Time: about one minute — much shorter than an online passport application.

U-C-I-T: User, Computer system, Interaction, Task. "U Can Interact with Tasks." Then add three task questions: How hard? What skill? How long?

Mistake: Calling the bank manager an "end user" of the ATM, or saying a task has only one property (difficulty).

Fix: The manager is usually a stakeholder. The person pressing the keys is the end user. A task always has complexity, knowledge and time — mention all three in long answers.

User interaction can happen in many different ways depending on the technology used. Learn every type with one clean example — board questions often ask you to "list and explain" five or more.

Worked example: a full JazzCash "Send Money" session, labelled by type.

  1. Open the app with a fingerprint → biometric.
  2. Home screen shows Send Money, Bill Payment, Load → you tap Send Money → menu-driven.
  3. Type the receiver's mobile number and the amount → form fill.
  4. The keypad vibrates on each tap → haptic.
  5. Optional: say "Send five hundred to Ali" then tap Confirm → multimodal (voice + touch).

Same idea on a school portal: menu-driven ("Date Sheet", "Fee Challan"), form fill (leave application), conversational (a chatbot "When is the chemistry practical?").

Fix: Voice is spoken commands only. Conversational includes typed chat (ChatGPT) as well as voice. Command-based means you type precise codes such as dir, not clicking a menu.

Interface Types, Environment and Feedback

Components v–vii complete the HCI picture: the look of the controls, the place where you use them, and the signals that tell you "it worked" or "try again".

v) User interface (UI)

The user interface is the medium through which humans interact with computer systems.

  • Graphical User Interface (GUI): visual elements such as windows, icons and menus (Windows OS, Android).
  • Command-Line Interface (CLI): text-based typing (MS-DOS, Linux terminal).
  • Touch interface: tapping, swiping, pinching (smartphones, tablets).
  • Voice User Interface (VUI): spoken commands (smart speakers).
  • Natural User Interface (NUI): gestures, body movements or immersive environments (VR headsets, Microsoft Kinect).

vi) Environment

The environment is the real-world setting where a person uses a computer system. It is shaped by:

  • Physical conditions — an ATM outdoors in sunlight, heat, noise, rain.
  • Social conditions — a quiet classroom, a crowded bazaar, privacy at a PIN pad.
  • Technical hardware — slow college Wi-Fi, a cracked phone screen, an old browser.

vii) Feedback

Feedback is the signal or response given to confirm or guide actions.

System feedback

Response from the computer: a "Loading…" bar, a "Message Sent!" tick, or an error sound.

User feedback

Input from the user to the system: tapping a "Submit" button, giving a 5-star rating.

Worked example: environment and feedback at a roadside ATM in Hyderabad.

Physical: noon sunlight washes out a dim screen — the GUI must use high contrast.

Social: people stand in a queue; the user needs a privacy shield around the PIN pad.

Technical: the printer may be out of paper, so on-screen "Take your cash" plus a beep still has to confirm the transaction.

System feedback: "Please wait", cash coming out, a printed or SMS receipt.

User feedback: the user presses Confirm, or later rates the bank's app 4 stars.

On a school portal during board-exam week, hundreds of students on weak Wi-Fi need a visible "Loading results…" bar. Silence after clicking "View marks" is poor system feedback.

GUI vs CLI vs NUI: GUI = pictures you click. CLI = words you type. NUI = body you move. VUI = voice you speak. Touch = fingers on glass.

Feedback rule: system talks back; user talks in.

Mistake: Mixing up system feedback with user feedback, or saying "environment" means only the software theme (dark mode).

Fix: A tick mark is system feedback. A 5-star rating is user feedback. Environment is the real world around the user — sun, noise, hardware — not the colour of the app (though a good UI will adapt to that world).

1.4 Importance of HCI ★ Golden

HCI ensures that machines are approachable and usable, turning complex technology into everyday tools. Good design makes technology simple and enjoyable (touchscreens). Poor design causes confusion and risks (unclear buttons on medical devices).

This is why HCI is not optional decoration. A confusing JazzCash confirm button can send money to the wrong number. A confusing school-portal "Submit" can lock a student out of an online exam. Importance questions in the board exam expect the five implications below, each with a sentence of explanation.

ImplicationDescription
1. Enhance productivityWell-designed interfaces help users work faster, make fewer mistakes, and achieve goals efficiently.
2. Promotes inclusivityAccessibility features (screen readers, voice commands) empower marginalized groups and ensure equal use of technology for everyone.
3. Drives innovationEmerging tech such as AR/VR, AI and IoT rely strictly on solid HCI principles for people to actually adopt and use them.
4. Economic impactUser-friendly products gain higher adoption rates, customer loyalty and market success, driving profits.
5. Ethical responsibilityResponsible design ensures technology respects user privacy, reduces bias and supports human well-being.

Worked example: good vs poor HCI, same task.

Task: send Rs 2,000 through JazzCash to a parent.

Good HCI: large "Send Money" button, amount shown in words and numbers, last 4 digits of the number repeated, fingerprint confirm, green "Successful" plus SMS. Result: fast, few mistakes, the user trusts the app (productivity + economic loyalty + ethics of clear consent).

Poor HCI: tiny grey buttons, no amount-in-words, no confirm screen, no tick. Result: wrong number, no idea if it worked, user deletes the app and goes back to a bank queue.

Medical contrast from the source: unclear buttons on a medical device are not just ugly — they are dangerous. That is why ethical responsibility sits in this table.

P-I-I-E-E: Productivity, Inclusivity, Innovation, Economic impact, Ethics. Sentence: "Please Include Innovative, Ethical Earnings."

Mistake: Writing only "HCI is important because it makes apps look nice."

Fix: Looks are a tiny part of UI. Importance answers must name productivity, inclusivity, innovation, money and ethics. Quote the medical-device warning if you have time.

1.5 Accessibility Principles

Accessibility principles are HCI guidelines that ensure digital systems are usable by all individuals, including people with diverse physical, sensory and cognitive abilities. They remove barriers to create equal access.

Accessibility is not a "special extra". It is how you make sure a student with low vision, a shopkeeper who cannot use a mouse, or a colour-blind driver can still complete the task.

PrincipleWhat it meansExample
1. High contrast (واضح رنگ)Make text and pictures stand out clearly against the background.Black text on a white background in a railway app, readable in bright sunlight.
2. Readable fonts (پڑھنے میں آسان تحریر)Clear, simple fonts with proper size and spacing.A school website using large Calibri so homework instructions are easy to read.
3. Colour-blind friendly design (رنگوں کی پہچان)Do not depend only on colours; also use symbols or text labels.A traffic app showing a red circle with a "STOP" label, not just a red colour.
4. Inclusive navigation (آسان نیویگیشن)Give users different ways to move around a system.A website that lets you use the Tab key to jump between buttons if you cannot use a mouse.
5. Multimodal detection (متعدد حسی ذرائع)Present information in different formats (sight, sound, touch).A video with text captions, or a phone vibrating to alert a user.

Worked example: audit a school portal against the five principles.

  1. Contrast: are result PDFs pale grey on white? Fail — use dark text.
  2. Fonts: is the fee-challan note in 9-point decorative script? Fail — large Calibri (or a similar simple font), as the source recommends.
  3. Colour: is "fail" only a red row with no word "Fail"? Fail — add the word, for colour-blind students.
  4. Navigation: can a student who cannot use a mouse reach "Download date sheet" with Tab + Enter? If not, fail.
  5. Multimodal: does the principal's video assembly have captions, and does a fee-due alert vibrate as well as flash? If not, deaf or busy students miss it.

The same five checks apply to an outdoor ATM (sunlight contrast) and to JazzCash (large numerals, Urdu labels, vibration on success).

1.6 Need Analysis for Interface Design

Need analysis is the process of identifying the gap between the existing situation and the desired outcome. In HCI it is done before designing a UI, so that you understand users' goals, tasks, abilities and expectations.

If you skip need analysis, you guess. Guessing is how a school portal ends up with 40 menus and no "Download date sheet" button on the home screen.

Steps in need analysis:

1. Identify the users→2. Collect information→3. Analyse needs→4. Prioritise→5. Develop solutions
  1. Identify the users: who exactly will use the system? (elderly people, teenagers, …)
  2. Information collection: gather data using interviews, questionnaires or observations.
  3. Analyse user needs: identify the core problems, goals and requirements.
  4. Prioritise needs: decide which features are the most critical to build first.
  5. Develop solutions: design a system that meets those verified needs.

Case study from the source: mobile banking app. Before building the app, the team interviews customers and discovers:

  • Users want secure login via Fingerprint / FaceID.
  • Elderly users need large buttons and simple navigation.
  • Users want support for both English and local languages.

Result: the UI is designed around these proven needs, which makes success more likely.

Apply the five steps to a college portal in Sindh.

  1. Users: Grade XI–XII students, parents, clerks, teachers. Not "everyone in Pakistan".
  2. Collect: interview 20 students; questionnaire to parents; watch a queue at the office on fee day.
  3. Analyse: students cannot find date sheets; parents cannot read English-only fee notices; clerks re-type the same form.
  4. Prioritise: (1) date sheet on home screen, (2) Urdu/Sindhi fee notice, (3) online leave form. Fancy animations wait.
  5. Solution: a simple portal with those three features, large buttons, bilingual labels.

Need analysis = gap finding. Write: Existing = what hurts now. Desired = what "done" looks like. The UI must close that gap, not decorate it.

Mistake: Starting in Figma on day one, then "asking users later".

Fix: Need analysis comes before UI design. Features you did not hear from users are guesses. Guessed features waste time and fail in exams when the question is "why do we analyse needs?"

نیڈ اینالیسس موجودہ صورتحال اور مطلوبہ نتیجے کے فرق کو یو آئی بنانے سے پہلے تلاش کرتا ہے۔ پانچ قدم: صارفین کی پہچان، انٹرویو یا مشاہدے سے معلومات، ضروریات کا تجزیہ، اہم خصوصیات کو ترجیح، پھر تصدیق شدہ ضرورتوں پر حل۔ موبائل بینکنگ کی مثال: فنگر پرنٹ لاگ اِن، بڑے بٹن، اور انگریزی کے ساتھ مقامی زبان۔

1.7 Human-Computer Interaction Problems ★ Golden

HCI problems are common difficulties that stop users from using technology smoothly. They happen when systems fail to meet human needs or cognitive limits. Cognitive limits means the mind can only hold and process so much at once — if an app asks you to remember three SMS codes at the same time, it has gone past that limit.

Board answers score well when you name the problem, explain it in one line, and give a concrete example (the source examples are safe; a matching local example is even better).

ProblemExplanation and example
Interface complexityToo many options or a confusing layout. Example: a shopping app with so many menus you cannot find the checkout button.
Poor feedbackThe system does not show if an action worked or failed. Example: clicking Submit and the screen freezes with no confirmation.
InconsistencyDifferent parts of the app look or act differently. Example: a magnifying-glass icon for Search on one page, a binocular icon on another.
Accessibility gapsDesign ignores people with disabilities. Example: a video tutorial platform without captions for deaf users.
Compatibility issuesInterface fails on different devices or browsers. Example: a website that works on a laptop but crashes on a mobile browser.
Contextual neglectThe system does not adjust to the user's situation. Example: a map app with no dark mode, blinding the driver at night.
High cognitive loadUsers must memorize too much or follow overly complex steps. Example: a registration system forcing you to remember three different SMS codes at once.
Trust deficiencyThe system feels unsafe or hides security cues. Example: an online store asking for credit cards but lacking an SSL/HTTPS lock icon.

Worked example: diagnose three local systems.

ATM: six tiny unmarked buttons beside the screen and no "Cash is coming" message → interface complexity + poor feedback. A user walks away while cash is still coming out.

JazzCash: "Send" is green on the home screen but orange on the confirm screen, and there is no padlock on the payment page → inconsistency + trust deficiency.

School portal: works in Chrome on a lab PC, blank on a student's Android browser; exam instructions are a caption-free video; login needs a portal password, an email OTP and an SMS OTP on the same screen → compatibility, accessibility gap, high cognitive load.

Once you can name the problem, Section 1.8 tells you how to fix it.

Eight problems in two families: (1) Screen problems — complexity, inconsistency, accessibility, compatibility, context. (2) Mind-and-trust problems — poor feedback, cognitive load, trust. If the user is lost, look at the screen family. If the user is scared or exhausted, look at the mind family.

Mistake: Writing "the app is bad" as an HCI problem, or mixing up cognitive load with compatibility.

Fix: Use the exact names from the table. Cognitive load is "too much to remember". Compatibility is "breaks on another device or browser".

ایچ سی آئی مسائل وہ رکاوٹیں ہیں جو سسٹم کو انسانی ضرورت یا ذہنی حد کے مطابق نہ ہونے پر پیدا ہوتی ہیں۔ آٹھ مسائل: انٹرفیس کی پیچیدگی، کمزور فیڈبیک، عدم مطابقت (ایک جیسا نہ ہونا)، رسائی کی کمی، آلہ/براؤزر سے ناسازگاری، سیاق کو نظرانداز کرنا، زیادہ ذہنی بوجھ، اور اعتماد کی کمی (جیسے HTTPS تالے کے بغیر کارڈ مانگنا)۔

1.8 Methods for Improving HCI

Once you can name an HCI problem, you fix it with a matching method. The source lists eight methods. Learn each with its tiny example — they pair almost one-to-one with Section 1.7.

  • Enhance usability: simplify navigation (a clear "Buy Now" button).
  • Strengthen accessibility: support disabilities (captions on videos).
  • Ensure consistency: keep layouts uniform (the same "Save" button everywhere).
  • Provide clear feedback: show action results ("Payment Successful" popup).
  • Improve adaptability: responsive design (the website scales to mobile screens).
  • Apply user-centred design (UCD): focus on real user goals and pilot-test.
  • Use multimodal interaction: allow touch, voice or gesture inputs combined.
  • Continuous testing: regularly update based on user feedback.

User-centred design (UCD) means the real user's goals drive every decision, and you trial (pilot-test) the design with those users instead of guessing in an office.

Responsive design means the layout rearranges itself for phone, tablet and PC, instead of staying a laptop-only page.

1.9 User Interface Design (UI vs UX) ★ Golden

UI design is the process of creating and organizing the visual elements of a software application so users can interact with it easily and efficiently.

1.9.1 Components of a user interface

ComponentRole
ButtonsClickable elements that perform actions (Submit, Cancel).
MenusLists of options for navigating the system.
IconsSmall symbols representing tools or actions.
Text fieldsBoxes where users type information (passwords, search).
Checkboxes / radio buttonsSelecting options in a form. Checkboxes allow several choices; radio buttons allow only one in a group.
SlidersAdjusting values along a bar (volume, brightness).

1.9.2 User experience (UX)

UX is the overall experience a user has while interacting with a system — how smooth, comfortable and satisfying it feels.

UI is what you see; UX is how it feels.

Source example: food-delivery app. The UI is the nice pictures of burgers and the red buttons. The UX is that the app loads fast, tracking works, and the food arrives without the user getting frustrated.

Local walk-through: JazzCash.

  • UI: green Send button, JazzCash logo, amount text field, fingerprint icon, menu of Bill / Load / Send.
  • UX: login in two seconds, no crash in a weak-signal bazaar, money arrives, SMS comes, the user feels safe and finished — or, if it hangs with no tick, the UX is bad even if the buttons look pretty.

School portal: UI = logo, "Login" button, date-sheet menu icon. UX = a student finds the chemistry practical date in under a minute on a phone, without calling the clerk.

Pretty UI + slow, confusing flow = poor UX. Plain UI + fast, clear flow = good UX.

Clothes vs day out. UI is the outfit (colours, buttons, icons). UX is the whole day (Did you arrive on time? Did you get wet? Were you stressed?). Examiners love the line: UI is what you see; UX is how it feels.

Mistake: Writing "UI and UX are the same" or "UX is just colours".

Fix: UI = visual components (buttons, menus, icons, fields, checkboxes, sliders). UX = overall smoothness, comfort and satisfaction, including speed and whether the task succeeds.

1.9.3–1.9.5 Wireframing, Figma and Prototypes

1.9.3 Wireframing is creating a basic visual outline or blueprint of a user interface before the final design. It plans the structure without focusing on colours or detailed graphics.

Low-fidelity wireframes (Lo-Fi)

A simple black-and-white sketch showing basic layout (boxes and lines). Used early to test and share ideas quickly. Focuses purely on structure and flow.

High-fidelity prototypes (Hi-Fi)

A detailed version that looks almost exactly like the final product. Includes real colours, fonts, images and interactive clickable buttons. Used for final user testing.

Lo-Fi sketch→Add details→Hi-Fi prototype

1.10 What is Figma?

Figma is a popular, free, web-based tool used to design and collaborate on UI and prototypes. It works directly in a web browser — you do not have to install a heavy program on the college lab PCs.

Creating a low-fidelity design in Figma

  1. Sign up: access the Figma workspace online.
  2. Create frames: select the Frame tool (for example an iPhone-sized frame) to act as your screen canvas.
  3. Add shapes: draw basic rectangles for buttons and circles for avatars.
  4. Add text: use the text tool to label buttons (for example, "Sign Up").
  5. Duplicate and edit: copy the frame to create the next screen (for example, the Home Screen).

1.9.5 Prototype design

A prototype is an early interactive model used to test design ideas before writing actual code. In Figma you use the Prototype tab to link screens together.

  • Set a Trigger (for example, "On Tap").
  • Set an Action (for example, "Navigate to Home Screen").

Real users can then click through the fake app to see if the navigation makes sense.

Worked example: JazzCash-style "Send Money" in Figma, without writing code.

  1. Lo-Fi on paper or in Figma: three frames — Home (boxes: Send, Bills, Load), Amount (text field + Next), Success (tick + Done).
  2. Show the sketch to two classmates. If they cannot find Send, move that box to the top. This is the point of Lo-Fi: cheap changes.
  3. In Figma, add a phone frame, rectangles, and labels. Duplicate the frame for Home, Amount and Success.
  4. Prototype tab: Trigger = On Tap of the Send rectangle; Action = Navigate to Amount. On Tap of Next → Success.
  5. Hand the laptop to a student. If they tap Bills by mistake, the navigation is still wrong — fix it before any programmer writes Java or Kotlin.

Lo-Fi = boxes. Hi-Fi = almost the real app. Prototype = clickable. Figma can hold all three stages. Trigger = what the user does. Action = where the fake app goes.

Mistake: Colouring a Lo-Fi wireframe, or thinking a Figma prototype is the finished coded app.

1.11 Test and Evaluate HCI (Evaluation Methods)

Testing checks if the product works properly (finding bugs). Evaluation determines whether it meets users' needs (usability). Together they improve the product before release.

A school portal can be "bug-free" (testing passed) and still unusable if students cannot find the date sheet (evaluation failed). You need both.

1.11.1 Evaluation methods

MethodDescription
SUS (System Usability Scale)A standard 10-item questionnaire used to calculate a score for how usable a system is.
NPS (Net Promoter Score)Asks users: "How likely are you to recommend this app to a friend?" Measures user loyalty.
Formative evaluationDone during development to collect early feedback and improve the design continuously.
Summative evaluationDone at the end of development to measure overall success against the original goals.
Expert reviewsUsability specialists review the app against established design standards (heuristics).

Heuristics are agreed rules of good design (for example "show system status", "be consistent"). An expert review checks the product against those rules without waiting for a huge user survey.

Worked example: when to use each method on a school portal.

  1. Week 2 of building: show the Lo-Fi to five students, change the menu. That is formative evaluation.
  2. A teacher who has studied HCI checks the screens against standard rules (Is feedback visible? Are labels consistent?). That is an expert review.
  3. After the portal is "finished", 30 students fill the 10 SUS questions. A low score means it is still hard to use, even if there are no crashes.
  4. Ask: "On a scale of 0–10, would you recommend this portal to a friend in another college?" That is NPS (loyalty).
  5. Compare the finished portal with the original goals (date sheet in one tap, bilingual fee notice). That is summative evaluation.

Formative = forming it (during). Summative = summing it up (end). SUS = 10 questions on usability. NPS = "Would you recommend it?" Expert = specialist vs heuristics.

Mistake: Saying testing and evaluation are the same, or mixing formative with summative.

Fix: Testing = bugs. Evaluation = does it meet human needs. Formative is during; summative is at the end against original goals.

1.11.2 Testing Methods (Usability, A/B, Automated) ★ Golden

Testing involves observing how the system behaves under specific conditions. Learn all seven methods. The three named in the heading — usability, A/B and automated — are the ones examiners ask most, but a complete answer also names the others.

  1. Usability testing: observing real users while they perform realistic tasks, to measure efficiency and satisfaction. If the user gets stuck, designers know where to fix the UI.
  2. Task-based testing: assigning a specific task (for example, "Find a product and add it to cart") and strictly measuring completion time, success rate and number of errors.
  3. Automated audits: software bots scan websites to detect technical issues such as broken links, slow loading speeds or low colour contrast.
  4. A/B testing: comparing two versions of an interface (A and B). Half the users see A, half see B. The one with more clicks or sign-ups wins.
  5. Compatibility testing: verifying the system on different browsers (Chrome, Safari), devices (mobiles, tablets, PCs) and operating systems.
  6. Performance and load testing: simulating high traffic (for example 10,000 users logging in at once) to see if speed drops or the server crashes.
  7. Keyboard and screen-reader walkthroughs: navigating using only the keyboard (Tab/Enter) or assistive technologies, to check accessibility for users with disabilities.

Worked example: test JazzCash "Send Money" and a school portal with numbers.

Usability testing. Sit beside five students. Task: "Send Rs 100 to this test number." Do not help. Note where they pause. If three people look for Confirm at the bottom and it is at the top, that is your UI fix.

Task-based testing (numbers). Same task for 10 students.

  • Success: 8 of 10 finish = success rate (8 ÷ 10) × 100 = 80%.
  • Times: 12, 14, 11, 40, 13, 12, 15, 50, 14, 12 seconds. The two slow times (40, 50) are the students who got stuck — they also made errors.
  • Errors: extra taps, wrong field. Count them. High errors + high time = poor HCI, even if most people eventually succeed.

A/B testing. Version A: green "Send" at the top. Version B: large orange "Send" under the amount. 200 users each. If B gets 140 completed payments and A gets 90, B wins. You keep B.

Automated audit. A bot reports 3 broken "Fee challan" links and low contrast on the portal's grey-on-grey marks table. Fix those before the board-exam rush.

Load testing. On result day, 10,000 students log in at 9:00 a.m. If the server crashes, performance testing failed — the UI never even appears.

Keyboard walkthrough. Unplug the mouse. Can a student Tab to "Download date sheet" and press Enter? If focus jumps randomly, accessibility testing failed.

Task-based three numbers: Time, Success, Errors. A/B: two versions, split users, the higher clicks/sign-ups win. Bot: links, speed, contrast. Load: many users at once. Keyboard: Tab and Enter only.

Mistake: Calling A/B testing "showing both designs to the same user and asking which is prettier".

Fix: Half the users see only A, half see only B. You measure behaviour (clicks, sign-ups), not a taste vote. Also: an automated audit does not replace watching real users — bots cannot see that a student is confused.

سات ٹیسٹنگ طریقے: یوزیبیلٹی (حقیقی صارف کو کام کرتے دیکھنا)، ٹاسک بیسڈ (وقت، کامیابی، غلطیاں ناپنا)، خودکار آڈٹ (بوت ٹوٹے لنک، سست رفتار، کم کنٹراسٹ ڈھونڈے)، اے/بی ٹیسٹ (آدھا صارف اے، آدھا بی؛ زیادہ کلک والا جیتے)، کمپیٹیبلٹی (براؤزر اور آلہ)، لوڈ ٹیسٹ (مثلاً دس ہزار لاگ اِن)، اور صرف کی بورڈ یا اسکرین ریڈر سے چل کر رسائی کی جانچ۔

Chapter 1 Review

Chapter summary

  • HCI (also CHI, MMI) designs technology to be easy, efficient and responsive to people, using five sensory channels: sight, touch (haptics), hearing, voice and spatial.
  • Traditional interaction uses mouse, keyboard, keypad or simple tap/swipe; natural interaction uses voice, face or body gesture without a mouse or keyboard.
  • HCI applications cover health care, banking, education, and networking/communication, each with a clear user benefit (remote care, quick secure money, better learning, global connection).
  • Four core components: user (end user vs stakeholder), computer system, user interaction, and task (complexity, knowledge, time).
  • Interaction types include direct manipulation, command-based, menu-driven, form fill, conversational, gesture, voice, context-aware, biometric, haptic and multimodal.
  • Interfaces: GUI, CLI, touch, VUI, NUI. Environment is physical, social and technical. System feedback is the machine's reply; user feedback is what the person sends in.
  • HCI matters for productivity, inclusivity, innovation, economic impact and ethical responsibility. Poor design (unclear medical buttons) creates risk.
  • Five accessibility principles: high contrast, readable fonts, colour-blind friendly labels, inclusive navigation, multimodal presentation.
  • Need analysis finds the gap before UI design: identify users, collect data, analyse, prioritise, develop solutions (banking case: FaceID, large buttons, local language).
  • Eight HCI problems: complexity, poor feedback, inconsistency, accessibility gaps, compatibility, contextual neglect, high cognitive load, trust deficiency.
  • Improve HCI with usability, accessibility, consistency, clear feedback, adaptability (responsive design), UCD and pilot tests, multimodal input, and continuous testing.
  • UI is the visual components (buttons, menus, icons, fields, checkboxes, sliders); UX is how the whole experience feels. UI is what you see; UX is how it feels.
  • Lo-Fi wireframes plan structure; Hi-Fi prototypes look real; Figma is a free browser tool; prototypes use Trigger (On Tap) and Action (navigate) before code.
  • Evaluation (SUS, NPS, formative, summative, expert/heuristic reviews) checks needs; testing (usability, task-based, automated, A/B, compatibility, load, keyboard/screen-reader) checks behaviour under conditions.

Short questions — model answers

  1. HCI is the design of technology to serve people; systems that are easy to use, efficient and responsive. Also called CHI (Computer-Human Interface) and MMI (Man-Machine Interaction). Channels: sight, touch, hearing, voice, spatial.
  2. Traditional uses standard I/O (mouse, keyboard, keypad, simple tap/swipe), e.g. an ATM keypad or typing on a school portal. Natural lets people interact without mouse or keyboard, using voice, face or gesture, e.g. FaceID or "Alexa, play a song".
  3. Health care: patient portals / telemedicine — remote access to medical services. Banking: mobile banking and ATMs — quick, secure transactions. Education: LMS and exam portals — better teaching, learning and engagement. Networking: WhatsApp, Zoom, social media — global connection and collaboration.
  4. User, computer system, user interaction, task. End users directly operate the system; stakeholders (managers, clients) have an interest in the outcome. Task characteristics: complexity, user knowledge, time requirement.
  5. GUI: windows, icons, menus (Windows, Android). CLI: typed commands (MS-DOS, Linux). VUI: spoken commands (smart speakers). NUI: gestures, body, immersive VR/Kinect.
  6. Enhance productivity; promote inclusivity; drive innovation (AR/VR, AI, IoT need solid HCI); economic impact (adoption, loyalty, profit); ethical responsibility (privacy, less bias, well-being).
  7. High contrast (black text on white in a railway app); readable fonts (large Calibri on a school site); colour-blind friendly (STOP label, not colour alone); inclusive navigation (Tab key); multimodal detection (captions or vibration).
  8. Need analysis finds the gap between the existing situation and the desired outcome, before UI design. Steps: identify users; collect information (interviews, questionnaires, observation); analyse needs; prioritise; develop solutions. Banking case: Fingerprint/FaceID login; large buttons and simple navigation for elderly users; English and local languages.
  9. UI is the visual elements so people can interact easily; UX is the overall smoothness, comfort and satisfaction. Food app: UI = burger pictures and red buttons; UX = fast load, working tracking, food arrives without frustration. Components: buttons, menus, icons, text fields, checkboxes/radio buttons, sliders.
  10. A/B testing shows version A to half the users and version B to the other half; the version with more clicks or sign-ups wins. An automated audit uses bots to scan for technical issues such as broken links, slow loading or low contrast; it does not split users between two designs.

Long questions — model answers

  1. Components through an outdoor ATM. User: end user = account holder; stakeholder = bank. Computer system: ATM hardware and bank software linking PIN input to cash output. Interaction: insert card, type PIN, choose Cash, confirm — intent becomes a response. Task: withdraw cash; low complexity, basic knowledge, about one minute (contrast with a long passport application). Interface: GUI on screen + keypad (traditional); possibly fingerprint (biometric). Environment: physical sunlight so contrast must be high; social queue so a privacy shield is needed; technical printer or network may fail. System feedback: "Please wait", cash, receipt/SMS. User feedback: pressing Confirm. If any part is weak (glare, no "cash coming" message), HCI fails even if the bank account is correct.
  2. Problems and methods. Interface complexity (buried checkout / hidden Send Money) → enhance usability (clear Buy Now / Send). Poor feedback (frozen Submit) → Payment Successful popup. Inconsistency (magnifying glass vs binoculars, or Send changing colour) → same Save/Send control everywhere. Accessibility gaps (no captions) → strengthen accessibility. Compatibility (laptop-only portal) → responsive/adaptability. Contextual neglect (map with no night mode; ATM unreadable in sun) → design for environment. High cognitive load (three SMS codes) → simplify steps. Trust deficiency (no HTTPS lock on JazzCash-style payment) → show security cues. Also apply UCD with a pilot test, multimodal input (voice + touch for elderly users), and continuous testing after launch.
  3. UI, UX, wireframe, Figma, prototype. UI design organises visual elements. Six components: buttons (Submit/Cancel), menus, icons, text fields, checkboxes/radio, sliders. UX = overall experience; UI is what you see, UX is how it feels; food-delivery pictures vs fast delivery. Lo-Fi: black-and-white boxes for structure; Hi-Fi: real colour, type, images, clickable controls for final tests. Figma: sign up in the browser; Frame tool for a phone canvas; rectangles and circles; text labels such as Sign Up; duplicate frames for the next screen. Prototype: before code, Prototype tab, Trigger = On Tap, Action = Navigate to Home. Sketch: header "LOGO / Portal", dashed Username and Password boxes, a LOGIN rectangle, "Forgot password?" — the Lo-Fi login in this chapter.
  4. Test vs evaluate, then methods. Testing finds bugs / whether it works; evaluation checks whether it meets user needs. SUS: 10-item usability score. NPS: "recommend to a friend?" (loyalty). Formative: during development. Summative: at the end vs original goals. Expert reviews: specialists vs heuristics. Usability testing: watch real users on realistic tasks. Task-based: time, success rate, errors (e.g. Add to cart in 12 s, success yes). Automated audit: bots find broken links, slow load, low contrast. A/B: half/half, more clicks win. Compatibility: Chrome/Safari, phone/tablet/PC, OS. Load: 10,000 logins at once. Keyboard/screen-reader: Tab/Enter and assistive tech. Result-day portal: load-test the 9:00 a.m. rush; usability-test "find chemistry marks"; automated audit for broken result links and grey-on-grey contrast; optional A/B of two home-screen layouts.
  5. Send Money for elderly users in Sukkur. Need analysis: (1) users = elderly Sindhi speakers, not teenagers; (2) collect: interviews and watch them at a JazzCash shop agent; (3) analyse: fear of wrong number, cannot read small English, glare on the phone; (4) prioritise: huge Sindhi "رقم بھیجیں", amount in words, confirm screen, high contrast; (5) solution: that UI, not a CLI. Accessibility: high contrast for sunlight, large simple fonts, not colour-only status, Tab/large targets, vibration + spoken confirm (multimodal). Interaction: menu-driven + form fill + biometric + haptic; optional voice. Evaluate formatively on paper then in Figma; expert review against heuristics; SUS and NPS before launch. Test: usability and task-based ("send Rs 100") measuring time/success/errors; A/B two button sizes; compatibility on cheap Androids; keyboard if a kiosk is used; do not skip a confirm screen — trust and feedback are the main risks.

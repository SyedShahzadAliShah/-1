5.1 Introduction to Computing Impacts

Computing means using computers and computer-based devices to store, process and share information. Today computing is an important part of your daily life. You use computers, mobile phones, the internet and smart devices almost everywhere — at home, in college and on the road.

In Pakistan, computing is becoming vital (very necessary) in:

  • Schools and colleges — online admissions, e-books, online results of the Sindh boards.
  • Hospitals — computerised patient records, digital X-rays, online appointment systems.
  • Banks — ATMs, mobile banking apps, Raast instant payments, JazzCash and Easypaisa wallets.
  • Farms — weather forecasts on mobile phones, soil sensors, tube-well control.
  • Industries — automatic machines in textile mills in Karachi, Hyderabad and Faisalabad.

An impact is the effect something has on people and society. Computing has positive impacts (speed, convenience, new jobs) and negative impacts (privacy risks, addiction, job loss). In this chapter you will study three key technologies first — AI, IoT and Data Analytics — and then their uses and impacts.

A day in Sara's life in Karachi — spot the computing:

Step 1. 7:00 am — Her phone unlocks by recognising her face (AI).

Step 2. 7:30 am — She books a ride on a ride-hailing app; GPS shows the car's location (internet + location data).

Step 3. 9:00 am — College attendance is marked by a biometric (fingerprint) machine (IoT).

Step 4. 2:00 pm — She pays her fee through a mobile banking app (computing in business).

Step 5. 8:00 pm — YouTube suggests a physics lecture based on what she watched yesterday (AI).

Conclusion: in a single day, Sara uses computing in at least five different fields without even thinking about it.

5.1.1 Artificial Intelligence (AI) ★ Golden

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is the ability of a computer system to perform tasks that normally need human thinking, such as recognising faces, understanding language and making decisions.

Understand the name in two parts:

  • Artificial — it is created by humans; it is not natural.
  • Intelligence — it can perform tasks that require thinking.

AI systems use data to make decisions. The more good data an AI system sees, the better it becomes at recognising patterns (repeating features) and predicting what to do.

Examples from the source:

  1. Face recognition on a mobile phone to unlock the screen.
  2. YouTube suggesting videos based on your interests.
  3. Chatbots answering questions on a website (for example, a bank's help chat).

More Pakistani examples: Google Maps predicting traffic on Shahrah-e-Faisal, Urdu voice typing on your phone, banks flagging a suspicious card transaction, and AI tools that translate English into Urdu.

AI in smart systems

AI rarely works alone. It often acts as the "brain" of a system, working together with the Internet of Things (IoT) and Data Analytics:

  • IoT devices gather data — they are the "eyes" (sensors).
  • Analytics sorts the data — it organises and studies it.
  • AI makes the final decision — it is the "brain".
  • An action follows — something happens in the real world.
IoT (Eyes) — collects data→Analytics — sorts data→AI (Brain) — decides→Action

Smart air-conditioner in a hot Hyderabad room — trace the system:

Step 1 (IoT – eyes). A temperature sensor reads the room temperature: 36 °C.

Step 2 (Analytics). The readings of the last 10 minutes are organised: 34, 35, 35, 36 °C — the room is getting hotter.

Step 3 (AI – brain). The AI compares this with the comfortable limit (say 26 °C) and with the fact that a person is in the room (motion sensor). Decision: "Turn the AC on."

Step 4 (Action). A switch turns the AC on automatically.

No human pressed a button — the system sensed, thought and acted.

Eyes – Sort – Brain – Do. IoT is the eyes, Analytics sorts, AI is the brain, and the actuator does the action. Think of a cricket fielder: his eyes see the ball (IoT), he judges speed and direction (analytics), decides to dive (AI), and his body moves (action).

Mistake: "AI means robots." Fix: Most AI is just software — YouTube suggestions, spam filters and face unlock have no robot body. A robot may use AI, but AI itself is the thinking software.

5.1.2 Internet of Things (IoT) & Components ★ Golden

The Internet of Things (IoT) is a system in which physical devices are connected to the Internet and can collect and share data to perform tasks automatically.

The "things" are ordinary objects — watches, cameras, cars, fans, water pumps — that have been given sensors and an internet connection so they can "talk" to each other and to us.

Source example: A smartwatch collects your heart-rate data and sends it to your mobile phone, where it is displayed.

Components of IoT systems

IoT relies on six foundational components to work properly.

#ComponentWhat it doesExamples
1SensorsDevices that collect information from the physical world. They are the "eyes and ears" of the system.Temperature sensor, motion sensor, fingerprint sensor, smoke sensor, soil moisture sensor
2ActuatorsDevices that perform physical actions in response to system commands. They convert digital signals into physical movement.Electric motor, switch, automatic door opener, water valve, fan controller
3ConnectivityThe connection that allows devices to send and receive data. Without it, sensors can't talk to the system.Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, ZigBee, 4G/5G
4Data ProcessingChanging raw data into useful information. Steps: collecting, organising, analysing and deciding.Checking if the room temperature is too hot to trigger the AC
5Cloud ComputingStoring data and using services over the internet instead of local hard drives. Handles massive IoT data.Google Drive, iCloud, online LMS
6User Interface (UI)The component that enables interaction between the user and the device. Helps users see information and give commands.Mobile apps, touch screens, voice commands

Understanding each component

  • Sensors turn something in the real world (heat, movement, a fingerprint, smoke, wetness of soil) into data. A smoke sensor in a Karachi shopping mall "smells" smoke and reports it.
  • Actuators do the opposite: they take a digital command and make something move or change. The water valve that opens, the motor that turns, the door that slides open at a bank entrance.
  • Connectivity is the "road" for data. Short distances use Bluetooth (watch to phone); a house uses Wi-Fi; low-power sensor networks use ZigBee; devices far away in fields use mobile networks (4G/5G from Jazz, Zong, Telenor/Ufone).
  • Data processing is where raw numbers ("36") become meaning ("too hot — turn on AC"). Its four steps are collect → organise → analyse → decide.
  • Cloud computing stores and processes the huge amount of data that thousands of sensors produce every second — far more than a single small device can hold.
  • User Interface is how you see the data and give orders — an app on your phone, a touch screen on a smart fridge, or saying "Hey Google, turn off the lights".

How the components connect

Sensor (Collects)→Wi-Fi (Connectivity)→Cloud (Processes)→Actuator (Acts)→User App (UI)

Smart fire alarm in a Karachi factory — identify all six components:

Step 1 — Sensor: A smoke sensor detects smoke near the machines.

Step 2 — Connectivity: It sends the reading over the factory Wi-Fi.

Step 3 — Cloud computing: The reading is stored on an online server along with the history of all sensors.

Step 4 — Data processing: The software checks: "Is the smoke level above the safe limit?" Yes → decide "Fire risk!"

Step 5 — Actuator: Water valves of the sprinklers open and the alarm bell switch turns on.

Step 6 — User interface: The manager's mobile app shows a red alert: "Smoke detected – Hall 3".

Sensor senses, Actuator acts. Both words give you the answer: sensor = see/sense (input), actuator = act (output). Remember the six components with "Some Ants Carry Data Carefully Up" — Sensors, Actuators, Connectivity, Data processing, Cloud, UI.

Mistake: Calling a fan controller or water valve a "sensor". Fix: Ask "does it measure something or do something?" A valve opens and closes — it does, so it is an actuator. A soil moisture sensor only measures, so it is a sensor.

انٹرنیٹ آف تھنگز (IoT) ایک ایسا نظام ہے جس میں مختلف آلات (جیسے گھڑیاں، کیمرے، گاڑیاں) انٹرنیٹ سے جڑے ہوتے ہیں اور خودکار طریقے سے ڈیٹا جمع اور شیئر کرتے ہیں۔ اس کے چھ بنیادی اجزاء ہیں: سینسرز (معلومات جمع کرتے ہیں)، ایکچویٹرز (جسمانی کام انجام دیتے ہیں جیسے موٹر چلانا یا دروازہ کھولنا)، کنیکٹیویٹی (وائی فائی، بلوٹوتھ)، ڈیٹا پروسیسنگ، کلاؤڈ کمپیوٹنگ، اور یوزر انٹرفیس (موبائل ایپ)۔

5.1.3 Data Analytics & Components

Data Analytics is the process of collecting, organising, cleaning, studying and presenting data to find useful information. It helps people make better decisions.

Data means raw facts and figures (for example, "72, 45, 88"). Information is data that has been processed so it has meaning ("Ali scored 72%; he passed"). Data Analytics is the bridge from data to information.

Source example: A college collects exam marks, calculates percentages, and identifies which students need extra help. This entire process is Data Analytics.

Components of Data Analytics

Data Analytics comprises five key components, which happen in order like a pipeline:

1. Collection→2. Storage→3. Cleaning→4. Analysis→5. Visualization
  1. Data Collection — Gathering raw information from multiple sources: websites, surveys, sensors, hospital records, bank transactions.
  2. Data Storage — Storing data safely and properly after collection. It can be stored in files, databases, spreadsheets or the cloud.
  3. Data Cleaning — Identifying and correcting errors in data. Raw data may contain:
    • Duplication of records (the same student entered twice),
    • Incorrect values (typos, e.g. marks "870" instead of "87"),
    • Missing files or records (a student's marks not entered),
    • Improper formats (dates written as 05/10/2026 in one place and "5 Oct 26" in another).
  4. Data Analysis — Studying the clean data to identify patterns, trends and relevant information for problem-solving (e.g. finding out which subjects students failed the most).
  5. Data Visualization — Creating visual representations of data (plots, charts, graphs) to provide valuable insights at a glance (e.g. a pie chart showing pass vs fail percentage).

A college in Sukkur analyses Physics test results (out of 50) for 6 students.

Step 1 — Collection: Marks are gathered from the teacher's register.

Roll NoNameMarks (raw)
1Ayesha42

3Hina35
3Hina35
4Kashif250

Step 2 — Storage: The marks are typed into a spreadsheet saved on Google Drive (cloud).

Step 3 — Cleaning: Hina's record is a duplicate → delete one copy. Kashif's 250 is an incorrect value (more than 50) → the register shows 25, so correct it. Sana's marks are missing → ask the teacher; she was absent, so mark her "absent".

Step 4 — Analysis: Clean marks: 42, 18, 35, 25. Pass mark is 17 (33% of 50 = 16.5, rounded up). All 4 passed. Percentages: Ayesha 84%, Bilal 36%, Hina 70%, Kashif 50%. Average = (42 + 18 + 35 + 25) ÷ 4 = 120 ÷ 4 = 30 marks = 60%. Bilal is closest to failing → needs extra help.

Step 5 — Visualization: A bar chart shows each student's percentage; Bilal's short bar makes the problem visible at a glance.

"Cows Sit Calmly And Vote" — Collection, Storage, Cleaning, Analysis, Visualization. Or think of cooking daal: buy the lentils (collect), keep them in a jar (store), pick out stones (clean), cook (analyse), serve beautifully on a plate (visualise).

Mistake: Skipping cleaning and going straight to analysis. Fix: Dirty data gives wrong answers. In the example, Kashif's wrong "250" would have made the average (42 + 18 + 35 + 250) ÷ 4 = 86.25 — more than 50, which is impossible. Always clean first.

5.2 Uses of IoT in Different Areas

IoT improves efficiency (doing more with less effort), saves time, and enhances the overall quality of life. It plays a significant role in developing modern infrastructure (the basic systems of a country — roads, power, water, hospitals). The source lists six areas.

1. Smart Cities

  • Traffic management: Sensors adjust traffic signals automatically to reduce congestion (crowding). Example: signals on a busy Karachi road stay green longer for the direction with more cars.
  • Street lighting: Lights turn ON/OFF based on daylight or movement, saving electricity.
  • Parking: Sensors guide drivers to empty parking spaces, e.g. in a large shopping mall car park.
  • Waste management: Smart bins send alerts when full, so trucks collect only full bins instead of driving to every bin.

2. Industrial Automation

  • Sensors check machine temperature and vibration to prevent breakdowns (called predictive maintenance).
  • Automated machines perform repeated tasks precisely.
  • Quality-control sensors check products during manufacturing — for example, in the textile industries of Sindh, cameras and sensors spot faults in cloth as it is woven.

3. Healthcare (Smart Health)

  • Smartwatches continuously measure heart rate and vital signs (pulse, oxygen level).
  • Remote monitoring devices send patient data directly to doctors — vital for rural areas of Pakistan, where a specialist may be hundreds of kilometres away (e.g. a heart patient in Tharparkar monitored by a doctor in Karachi).
  • Emergency alert devices inform hospitals instantly, for example when an elderly person falls.

4. Education (Smart Learning)

  • Smart classrooms: digital boards and connected projectors.
  • Attendance systems: biometric automated attendance for students and teachers (used in many Sindh government schools and colleges).
  • Resource management: automated classroom lighting and AC control — off when the room is empty.

5. Home Automation

  • Smart lighting: controlled remotely via mobile apps.
  • Security: real-time monitoring via Wi-Fi cameras — you can watch your house gate on your phone.
  • Voice control: using assistants (like Alexa or Google Assistant) to control TVs and appliances.

6. Smart Farming

Smart farming is crucial for agriculture in Pakistan (especially Sindh and Punjab) to save water and improve yields (the amount of crop produced).

  • Soil moisture sensors: check if crops actually need water.
  • Automated irrigation: turns water pumps ON/OFF based on soil data.
  • Drones: monitor crop health from above, spotting disease or dry patches.

Smart farming process:

Soil Sensor→Cloud AI (Needs water?)→Water Pump

A cotton farmer near Nawabshah uses smart irrigation:

Step 1. A soil moisture sensor in the field reads 18% moisture.

Step 2. It sends the reading over the 4G network to a cloud service.

Step 3. The cloud AI checks: cotton needs at least 25% moisture, and the weather forecast shows no rain → decision "Needs water: YES".

Step 4. The actuator (pump switch) turns the tube-well ON.

Step 5. When moisture reaches 30%, the AI decides "enough" and the pump turns OFF.

Result: water is used only when needed — important because Sindh faces water shortages — and the farmer sees everything on his phone app.

Remember the six areas with "CHIEF H": Cities, Health, Industry, Education, Farming, Homes.

Mistake: Giving only the name of the area ("smart cities") in an exam answer. Fix: Always add what IoT does there — e.g. "smart bins send alerts when full" — and, if possible, a Pakistani example.

5.2.1 Scope of AI in Education in Pakistan

Artificial Intelligence is gradually becoming an important part of Pakistan's education system, improving teaching, learning and administration (managing the school: admissions, records, timetables).

Practical uses:

  • Learning Management Systems (LMS) — online platforms (e.g. Google Classroom, Moodle) where teachers post lessons and assignments; AI features can recommend lessons to each student.
  • Automated grading and digital testing — MCQ tests marked instantly by computer.
  • AI-based career counselling — software that studies a student's marks and interests and suggests suitable fields.

Benefits:

  • Helps students learn at their own speed.
  • Identifies students who need extra help.
  • Supports students with disabilities (Assistive AI), e.g. text-to-speech for a visually impaired student.

Major challenges in Pakistan:

  • Limited internet access in rural areas.
  • Lack of computers and digital devices for all students.
  • Need for proper teacher training to use AI tools.
  • Data privacy and security issues — students' personal data must be protected.

5.3 Information Sources (Primary, Secondary, Tertiary) ★ Golden

Information sources are ways to find knowledge and data. The internet has vast information, but not all of it is accurate. Sources are divided into three main categories.

1. Primary sources give original, firsthand information straight from people, events or official records. They are highly authentic (genuine).
Examples: interviews, photographs, diaries, NADRA official records.

2. Secondary sources contain information that has already been collected and explained by someone else. They summarise or analyse primary data.
Examples: textbooks, news reports, research papers, Pakistan Bureau of Statistics reports.

3. Tertiary sources provide highly summarised and organised information derived from both primary and secondary sources. They are used for quick reference.
Examples: encyclopedias, Wikipedia, dictionaries, handbooks.

Many examples, sorted

Primary (firsthand)Secondary (explained by others)Tertiary (summarised, quick reference)
Interview with a flood victim in DaduNewspaper report on the 2022 floodsEncyclopedia entry "Floods in Pakistan"
Photograph taken at an eventTextbook chapter explaining the eventWikipedia article
Personal diary or letterBiography written by a historianDictionary
NADRA official records (CNIC data)Pakistan Bureau of Statistics report analysing census dataHandbook / almanac of facts
Original survey responsesResearch paper analysing the surveyIndex or list of research papers
Sensor readings from an IoT weather stationTV news analysis of the weatherA school "fact sheet" on climate
Quaid-e-Azam's original speech recordingArticle discussing the speechEncyclopedia summary of his life

When do sources become unreliable?

In the digital age you must check facts carefully. A source is unreliable (cannot be trusted) if:

Source typeReasons for unreliability
PrimaryInformation is incomplete, data is recorded incorrectly, or false information was deliberately provided.
SecondaryThe information is outdated, lacks proper references/citations, or has not been verified by an expert.
TertiaryComes from an unknown/unverified website, or the information is oversimplified and misses key facts.

Rule of thumb (Author – Date – References): If an online article does not state the author's name, the date of publication, or list its references, it should not be trusted for academic work. Remember "ADR" — like checking a bus has a driver, a route and a ticket before you board.

Classify these — worked solutions:

(a) Your grandfather's handwritten diary from 1947. Written by a person who lived through the event → firsthand → Primary.

(b) A Dawn newspaper article analysing the budget. A journalist collected facts and explained them → Secondary.

(c) An Oxford English–Urdu dictionary. Organised, summarised reference built from many sources → Tertiary.

(d) A Bureau of Statistics report on literacy in Sindh. It analyses census (primary) data → Secondary.

(e) A photo you took of a road accident. Original record of the event → Primary.

Method: Ask (1) "Was the creator there / is it the original record?" → Primary. (2) "Is someone explaining or analysing original data?" → Secondary. (3) "Is it a quick-reference summary of many sources?" → Tertiary.

Exercise — classify these as Primary (P), Secondary (S) or Tertiary (T):

1. A video interview with a cricketer after a match.

2. Your Computer Science textbook.

3. Wikipedia page on "Mohenjo-daro".

4. A birth certificate issued by the union council.

5. A research paper reviewing ten studies on smog in Lahore.

6. A handbook of physics formulas.

7. Original exam answer sheets of students.

8. A TV news report summarising a court decision.

Answers: 1. P 2. S 3. T 4. P 5. S 6. T 7. P 8. S

Mistake: Thinking "primary" means "most important" or "tertiary" means "useless". Fix: The names describe how far the information is from the original event. Primary = straight from the source; tertiary = summarised summary. Each is useful for a different job. Also, Wikipedia is tertiary — not primary — because it summarises other sources.

معلومات کے ذرائع علم اور ڈیٹا تلاش کرنے کے طریقے ہیں، جنہیں تین اقسام میں تقسیم کیا جاتا ہے۔ پرائمری ذرائع براہِ راست اور اصل معلومات دیتے ہیں (جیسے انٹرویو، تصاویر، نادرا کا ریکارڈ)۔ سیکنڈری ذرائع وہ ہیں جن میں کسی اور نے پرائمری معلومات کی وضاحت یا تجزیہ کیا ہو (جیسے درسی کتابیں، خبریں)۔ ٹرشیری ذرائع خلاصہ شدہ معلومات دیتے ہیں جیسے انسائیکلوپیڈیا، وکی پیڈیا اور لغت۔ جس آن لائن مضمون میں مصنف کا نام، تاریخ یا حوالہ جات نہ ہوں اس پر بھروسہ نہ کریں۔

Typical questions: "Differentiate between primary, secondary and tertiary sources with two examples each" (long/short), "Classify the following sources…" (MCQ/short), and "When does a source become unreliable?"

5.4 Impacts of Computing in Various Fields

Computing has drastically changed society. It makes life faster and more connected, but it also brings challenges like privacy risks and technology addiction. For every field, think of both sides.

FieldPositive impact (+)Negative impact (−)
EducationOnline classes, e-books and learning apps make studying highly flexible and accessible.Excessive screen time reduces attention spans and causes physical health issues (eye strain, back pain).
CommunicationEmails and video calls allow instant, global communication at almost zero cost (e.g. a WhatsApp video call to a relative in Dubai).People interact less in person, leading to social isolation and reduced face-to-face skills.
BusinessOnline shopping, digital banking and automation dramatically improve speed and efficiency.Traditional manual jobs are lost to machines; risk of severe cyber-attacks on banks.

AgricultureSmart irrigation and weather forecasting massively increase crop yields.Poor internet access in rural areas means many farmers cannot use these tools.

Smart and responsible use

  • Protect personal information and use strong passwords (long, with letters, numbers and symbols; never share your OTP or CNIC details with callers).
  • Do not share fake news or unverified WhatsApp forwards.
  • Balance screen time with physical sports and activities.

Scenario analysis — a bank in Karachi moves to mobile banking:

Step 1 — Identify the field: Business (banking).

Step 2 — Positive: Customers transfer money in seconds from home; no queue at the branch; the bank processes more transactions with fewer staff (speed and efficiency).

Step 3 — Negative: Some cashier jobs are no longer needed (job loss); hackers may attempt cyber-attacks or phishing SMS to steal accounts.

Step 4 — Responsible use: Customers should use strong passwords, never share OTPs, and report suspicious messages.

For the five fields, remember "E-C-B-H-A" — Education, Communication, Business, Healthcare, Agriculture — and always write one + and one − for each.

Mistake: Writing only positive impacts in a "discuss the impacts" question. Fix: "Impact" means both sides. Examiners expect a balanced answer: advantages, disadvantages, and how to use technology responsibly.

5.5 Assistive Technologies ★ Golden

Assistive technologies are devices, software and systems that help people with disabilities or functional limitations (difficulty in seeing, hearing, moving, speaking, remembering, etc.). They allow more people to participate equally in school, work and society.

There are four categories:

CategoryPurposeExamples
1. Mobility aidsHelp people move from one place to another.Smart wheelchairs, stair lifts
2. Sensory aidsSupport vision and hearing to improve interaction.Hearing aids, screen readers (VoiceOver), Braille displays
3. Communication aidsAssist individuals with speech difficulties to express themselves.Speech-generating devices, text-to-speech apps
4. Cognitive / learning aidsHelp people with memory, reading and understanding.Audiobooks, spell-checkers, digital reminder apps
Mobility·Sensory·Communication·Cognitive
  • A screen reader is software that reads aloud the text on a screen, so a blind person can use a phone or computer. VoiceOver is Apple's screen reader; Android has TalkBack.
  • A Braille display is a device with small pins that rise and fall to form Braille dots, so a blind user can read screen text with their fingers.
  • A speech-generating device speaks out words that a person types or selects on a screen.
  • A smart wheelchair can be controlled by a joystick, voice or head movement and may avoid obstacles with sensors.

Scenario — choose the right assistive technology:

(a) Ahmed, a student in Hyderabad, is blind and wants to read his e-textbook. → Sensory aid: a screen reader or Braille display.

(b) Mrs Rashida cannot climb the stairs of her house after a knee operation. → Mobility aid: stair lift (or smart wheelchair).

(c) Zain cannot speak clearly due to a medical condition but can type. → Communication aid: text-to-speech app or speech-generating device.

(d) Nani often forgets to take her medicine. → Cognitive aid: digital reminder app.

(e) Usman has dyslexia (difficulty reading). → Cognitive/learning aid: audiobooks and spell-checkers.

Method: identify the limitation (moving, seeing/hearing, speaking, memory/reading) → choose the matching category.

Four categories = "My Small Cat Cries": Mobility, Sensory, Communication, Cognitive. Link each to a body function: legs (mobility), eyes/ears (sensory), mouth (communication), mind (cognitive).

Mistake: Putting text-to-speech and screen readers in the same category every time. Fix: In the source, the screen reader is a sensory aid (supports vision), while a text-to-speech app is listed as a communication aid (helps a person with speech difficulty express themselves). Decide by whose problem it solves.

معاون ٹیکنالوجیز (Assistive Technologies) وہ آلات، سافٹ ویئر اور نظام ہیں جو معذور افراد کی مدد کرتے ہیں تاکہ وہ اسکول، کام اور معاشرے میں برابر حصہ لے سکیں۔ ان کی چار اقسام ہیں: نقل و حرکت میں مدد (اسمارٹ وہیل چیئر)، حسی مدد (سماعتی آلہ، اسکرین ریڈر، بریل ڈسپلے)، گفتگو میں مدد (اسپیچ جنریٹنگ ڈیوائس) اور ذہنی و تعلیمی مدد (آڈیو بکس، اسپیل چیکر، یاد دہانی ایپس)۔

Expect: "What are assistive technologies? Explain their categories with examples" (long), "Name two sensory aids" (short), or "A screen reader is an example of ___" (MCQ).

For full marks write the definition (devices, software and systems; help people with disabilities; equal participation), then all four categories each with purpose + two examples. Finish with one line on importance (equal access, independence, inclusion).

1. Which category does a hearing aid belong to?

2. Give two examples of cognitive/learning aids.

3. Define assistive technologies in one sentence.

Answers: 1. Sensory aids. 2. Any two: audiobooks, spell-checkers, digital reminder apps. 3. Devices, software and systems that help people with disabilities or functional limitations participate equally in school, work and society.

5.5.1 Importance of Assistive Technologies

Assistive technologies are important for three main reasons:

  1. Equal access to education: Screen readers allow blind students to read digital textbooks equally with their peers (classmates).
  2. Independence in daily life: Mobility aids reduce the need for constant human assistance — a person in a smart wheelchair can go to the market without someone pushing.
  3. Social inclusion: They break down barriers, allowing individuals to work, study and socialise confidently.
Equal Access→Independence→Inclusion

How one tool changes a life: A visually impaired student in Karachi uses a screen reader on her laptop. Equal access — she reads the same e-books as her class. Independence — she no longer needs someone to read to her. Inclusion — she joins the class WhatsApp group, takes online tests and later gets a job, contributing to society.

Remember "AII": Access, Independence, Inclusion.

معاون ٹیکنالوجیز کی اہمیت تین باتوں میں ہے: تعلیم تک برابر رسائی (نابینا طلبہ اسکرین ریڈر سے کتابیں پڑھ سکتے ہیں)، روزمرہ زندگی میں خود مختاری (کسی کی مسلسل مدد کی ضرورت کم ہوتی ہے)، اور سماجی شمولیت (لوگ اعتماد کے ساتھ پڑھ، کام اور میل جول کر سکتے ہیں)۔

1. State three reasons why assistive technologies are important.

2. How do mobility aids support independence?

Answers: 1. Equal access to education, independence in daily life, social inclusion. 2. They reduce the need for constant human assistance.

Long questions — model answers

  1. Definition: IoT is a system in which physical devices are connected to the internet and collect and share data to perform tasks automatically. Components: (1) Sensors — collect information from the physical world, e.g. temperature, motion, fingerprint, smoke, soil moisture sensors. (2) Actuators — perform physical actions in response to commands, converting digital signals to movement, e.g. motor, switch, door opener, water valve, fan controller. (3) Connectivity — lets devices send/receive data, e.g. Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, ZigBee, 4G/5G. (4) Data processing — changes raw data into useful information through collecting, organising, analysing and deciding, e.g. checking if a room is too hot to switch on the AC. (5) Cloud computing — stores data and provides services over the internet, handles massive data, e.g. Google Drive, iCloud, LMS. (6) User interface — lets users see information and give commands, e.g. mobile apps, touch screens, voice commands. Diagram: Sensor (collects) → Wi-Fi → Cloud (processes) → Actuator (acts) → User App (UI).
  2. Table: IoT — network of physical devices connected to the internet; purpose: data collection and automation; works with hardware; output: raw data from environment. AI — technology enabling machines to think and decide; purpose: intelligent decisions and predictions; works with smart software; output: smart decisions or actions. Data Analytics — process of examining data to find patterns; purpose: extracting meaningful insights; works with data (numbers, records); output: reports, graphs, insights. Working together: IoT is the eyes that gather data, Analytics sorts it, AI is the brain that decides. Example: a temperature sensor reads 36 °C (IoT) → recent readings are organised showing the room is heating up (Analytics) → AI decides the room is too hot (AI) → a switch turns on the AC (action).
  3. (1) Collection: gather marks of all Maths tests, attendance and survey responses from students. (2) Storage: keep them safely in a spreadsheet or database, e.g. on the cloud. (3) Cleaning: remove duplicate records, correct typing errors (e.g. 120 out of 100), fill or mark missing records, and make formats (dates, roll numbers) consistent. (4) Analysis: study the clean data to find patterns — e.g. which chapters most students got wrong, whether low attendance links to failing. (5) Visualization: present results in bar charts or a pie chart of pass vs fail so the principal sees the problem at a glance and plans extra classes for weak chapters.
  4. Primary: original, firsthand, highly authentic (interviews, photographs, diaries, NADRA records). Secondary: collected and explained by someone else, summarises/analyses primary data (textbooks, news reports, research papers, Bureau of Statistics reports). Tertiary: highly summarised and organised from primary and secondary sources for quick reference (encyclopedias, Wikipedia, dictionaries, handbooks). Classification: (i) secondary, (ii) primary, (iii) tertiary, (iv) primary, (v) secondary, (vi) tertiary. Unreliability: primary — incomplete, recorded incorrectly, or deliberately false; secondary — outdated, no references/citations, not verified by an expert; tertiary — unknown/unverified website, or oversimplified and missing key facts. Rule: do not trust an article without author, date or references.
  5. Definition: devices, software and systems that help people with disabilities or functional limitations participate equally in school, work and society. Categories: (1) Mobility aids — help people move; smart wheelchairs, stair lifts. (2) Sensory aids — support vision and hearing; hearing aids, screen readers (VoiceOver), Braille displays. (3) Communication aids — help people with speech difficulties express themselves; speech-generating devices, text-to-speech apps. (4) Cognitive/learning aids — help with memory, reading, understanding; audiobooks, spell-checkers, reminder apps. Importance: equal access to education (screen readers let blind students read digital textbooks), independence in daily life (mobility aids reduce need for constant help), social inclusion (people work, study and socialise confidently). Careers: any two of assistive software developer (speech-to-text tools), biomedical engineer (artificial limbs, hearing aids), UX/UI accessibility expert (websites usable by visually impaired users).
  6. Introduction: computing makes life faster and more connected but brings privacy risks and addiction. Education: + online classes, e-books and learning apps make study flexible and accessible; − excessive screen time reduces attention span and harms physical health. Healthcare: + digital patient records and telemedicine let doctors treat remote patients accurately (e.g. rural Sindh); − patient data privacy is at risk if hospital servers are hacked. Agriculture: + smart irrigation and weather forecasting greatly increase yields; − poor rural internet means many farmers cannot use these tools. Responsible use: protect personal information and use strong passwords; do not share fake news or unverified forwards; balance screen time with sports and physical activity. Conclusion: benefits are large if technology is used wisely.

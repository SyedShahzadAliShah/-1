6.1 Introduction to Digital Literacy

Digital literacy is the ability to understand and use digital devices, the internet, and online tools in a safe, effective, and responsible manner.

Look at the three key words:

  • Safe – you protect yourself and others online (for example, you do not share passwords or private details).
  • Effective – you get the job done well (for example, you find the right information quickly).
  • Responsible – you use information honestly and fairly (for example, you do not copy someone else's work or spread false news).

In our increasingly digital world, digital literacy is essential for everyone, not only for computer experts. It enables you to:

  • find information,
  • communicate with others,
  • produce digital content, and
  • solve problems through technology.

Did you know? Digital literacy is not only using devices; it is using digital information safely and wisely. A digitally literate student can:

Locate information→Organise it properly→Turn it into something useful→Share it responsibly

Is this student digitally literate?

Ali can type fast and plays games on his phone all day. When his teacher asks him to find the population of Karachi, he copies the first number he sees on a random website and does not check where it came from.

Step 1 – Can he use devices? Yes. He has basic skills.

Step 2 – Can he locate and judge information? No. He did not check whether the website was reliable.

Step 3 – Conclusion: Ali has computer skills but is not yet fully digitally literate. A digitally literate student would compare two or three trusted sources (for example, the Pakistan Bureau of Statistics website) before using the number.

Remember the four verbs of a digitally literate student: Locate – Organise – Create – Share ("LOCS": think of a lock that keeps your digital life safe).

Mistake: "Digital literacy means knowing how to use a computer." Fix: Using a computer is only the start. Digital literacy also means finding, judging, creating and sharing information safely and responsibly.

6.2 Digital Literacy in the Information Age

The Information Age is the period we live in now, where vast amounts of data are produced, shared and accessed instantly through the internet, mobile devices and digital platforms (websites, apps, social media).

Simply having access to technology is not enough. Owning a smartphone does not make you good at using information. You must develop the skills to understand, manage and use information wisely.

Digital literacy goes beyond basic computer skills. It involves three kinds of ability:

AbilityWhat it meansExample
TechnicalKnowing how to operate devices and software.Creating a Google Form; using a spreadsheet.
Cognitive (thinking)Thinking critically about information.Checking whether a news story is true before believing it.
SocialCommunicating and behaving well with others online.Being polite in a class WhatsApp group; respecting privacy.

Together these abilities help you function successfully in a digital society.

Applying the three abilities. A video claims "Drinking hot water cures dengue." What does a digitally literate student do?

Technical: Uses a search engine to search dengue treatment site:.gov.

Cognitive: Notices the video has no doctor or source, and that official health websites do not support the claim. Concludes it is false.

Social: Does not forward the video, and politely tells the family group that it is not reliable.

Three abilities = "Tech, Think, Talk": Technical, Cognitive (think), Social (talk with others).

Mistake: Thinking that "more information" automatically means "better information". Fix: In the Information Age the problem is not finding some information; it is choosing the right, reliable information from too much.

6.3 Types of Data ★ Golden

Data means facts, numbers, words or observations that we collect. The data gathered through a survey, an investigation or a classroom project can be described in two ways: qualitatively or quantitatively. Knowing the difference is critical, because each type is analysed in a different way.

6.3.1 Qualitative Data

Qualitative data describes what people think, feel, notice or experience. It is non-numerical and descriptive: it is made of words, not numbers. The word comes from quality (what something is like).

Examples: students' opinions about online classes; descriptions of study habits; observation notes ("The students looked bored after 20 minutes").

6.3.2 Quantitative Data

Quantitative data deals with numbers. The word comes from quantity (how much or how many). It is useful when you need to count, measure, compare, calculate averages, or display results in charts.

Examples: number of students using the internet daily; marks obtained in a test; time spent online in hours.

6.3.3 Comparing Qualitative and Quantitative Data

Type of dataNatureExamplesBest used for
QualitativeDescriptive, non-numericalOpinions, reasons, behaviourUnderstanding why and how something happens
QuantitativeNumerical, countable or measurableHours, scores, percentages, totalsComparing results and making graphs/charts

Going a little further (background). Quantitative data is sometimes split into two kinds:

  • Discrete data can only take separate, countable values, e.g. number of students in a class (35, 36 – never 35.5).
  • Continuous data can take any value in a range and is measured, e.g. height (162.4 cm) or time online (2.75 hours).

Also note: a question with fixed categories (e.g. "Which activity do you use your phone for most? Lectures / Notes / Social media / Games") gives categories that you can count. In this chapter's case study such answers are treated as quantitative (categories), because you count how many students chose each option.

6.4.1 – 6.4.2 Interviews and Surveys ★ Golden

6.4.1 Interviews

An interview is a planned conversation used to gather information directly from a person.

  • It may be one-to-one (you and one person) or in a small group.
  • Questions are planned in advance, so you do not forget anything important.
  • Answers are usually qualitative (opinions, reasons, experiences).

Strength: you get deep, detailed answers and can ask follow-up questions ("Why do you say that?"). Weakness: it takes a lot of time, so you can only interview a few people.

6.4.2 Surveys / Questionnaires

A survey (or questionnaire) is useful when the same set of questions needs to be asked to many people.

  • It may be on paper or created with digital tools such as Google Forms or Microsoft Forms.
  • Note: online survey tools automatically collect responses and display basic charts, which makes data analysis much easier.

6.4.3 – 6.4.5 Prototypes, Observation and Simulations ★ Golden

6.4.3 Prototypes

A prototype is a simple early version of a product, model or solution. It allows an idea to be tested before time and effort are spent on the final version. Users try the prototype and give feedback, and this feedback becomes useful data for improvement.

Example: Before building a full school app, you draw the screens on paper (or make a few clickable screens in a design tool) and ask five classmates to try "finding the timetable". You note where they get confused.

6.4.4 Observation

In observation, the researcher watches what people do or what happens in a situation and records the results carefully. No direct questions are asked.

The data may be qualitative (written notes, e.g. "students push in the queue") or quantitative (counts, e.g. "42 students bought samosas in 10 minutes").

Why use it? People sometimes say one thing and do another. Observation shows what really happens.

6.4.5 Simulations

A simulation is a computer-based model of a real situation. Variables (the things that can change, like speed, temperature or number of people) can be changed to see what happens.

Simulations are highly useful when real experiments are costly, risky or difficult to perform.

Examples: a flight simulator for training pilots (real crashes are dangerous); a traffic simulation to test what happens if a new signal is added (changing real roads is costly); a weather model to predict a cyclone (you cannot experiment with real weather).

Choosing the right method. Which method would you use?

(a) "How many cars cross an intersection at 8 AM?" → Observation. Stand at the corner from 8:00 to 8:15 and tally each car. Asking drivers would be impossible and unnecessary; you just count.

(b) "How do students feel about the new cafeteria food?" → Survey or interview. Feelings are inside people's heads, so you must ask them. Use a survey for many students, or interviews for deeper reasons.

(c) "Will a new college website menu be easy to use?" → Prototype. Build a rough version and let users try it.

(d) "What happens to traffic if the bridge is closed for repairs?" → Simulation. Closing a real bridge to test is costly and disruptive.

Watch it → Observation. Ask it → Survey/Interview. Try it → Prototype. Model it → Simulation.

Mistake: Saying that observation involves asking people questions. Fix: In observation, no direct questions are asked – the researcher only watches and records. Also, a prototype is not the finished product; it is an early test version.

6.5 Primary and Secondary Data ★ Golden

Whenever you use data, it is important to know where it came from. The source affects how much control the researcher had over collecting it, and how confidently the results can be used.

6.5.1 Primary Data

Primary data is collected first-hand by the researcher for a particular purpose. It is gathered directly from people, events or situations, rather than taken from an already published source.

Sources: interviews, surveys, observations, experiments.

6.5.2 Secondary Data

Secondary data has already been collected by someone else and is being used again for a new purpose. It is taken from existing archives (stored collections of information).

Sources: books, research articles, official reports, web statistics, online databases.

6.5.3 Difference Between Primary and Secondary Data

AspectPrimary DataSecondary Data
SourceCollected directly by the researcher.Already collected by others.
ExamplesSurvey responses, interviews, observations.Reports, articles, websites, databases.
PurposePrepared for a specific study.Reused for a new purpose.
ControlHigh control over questions and collection process.Limited control because the data already exists.

Primary or secondary? Hira is researching "Internet use among students in Hyderabad".

(a) She sends a Google Form to 50 students in her college. → Primary (she collected it herself, for this study).

(b) She uses a PTA (Pakistan Telecommunication Authority) report on the number of broadband users in Pakistan. → Secondary (PTA collected it for its own purpose; Hira reuses it).

(c) She counts how many students use phones in the library during one hour. → Primary (her own observation).

(d) She quotes a statistic from a research article. → Secondary.

Why use both? Her primary data tells her about her own college; the secondary data lets her compare it with the national picture.

Primary = "1st hand" (you collect it first). Secondary = "2nd hand" (like a second-hand book – someone else used it first).

Mistake: Thinking data from a website is always primary because "I found it myself". Fix: What matters is who collected the data. If someone else collected it and you only found it, it is secondary.

6.6 Designing a Data-Collection Approach

Before collecting data, a researcher must have a plan. A plan makes sure that the information gathered is accurate, relevant (related to the question) and useful. Follow these eight steps in order:

  1. Define the research question clearly. What exactly do you want to find out?
  2. Decide the type of data needed: quantitative, qualitative, or both.
  3. Select suitable methods (survey, interview, observation).
  4. Choose the sample or target group – the people who will give you data.
  5. Prepare the tools such as forms, questions and observation sheets.
  6. Collect the data carefully and accurately.
  7. Store the data digitally in a spreadsheet and keep a backup.
  8. Follow ethical practice and respect privacy. Consent is mandatory (you must get permission).
Question→Data type→Method→Sample→Tools→Collect→Store + backup→Ethics

Applying the eight steps. Topic: water wastage in your college.

1. Question: "How much water is wasted at the college taps during break time, and why?"

2. Data type: Both – quantitative (number of taps left running, minutes) and qualitative (reasons students give).

3. Methods: Observation (count running taps) + a short survey (ask about habits).

4. Sample: All 12 taps in the college; 30 students from different classes for the survey.

5. Tools: An observation sheet with columns Tap no. / Time / Left running? (Y/N) / Minutes; a Google Form with 5 questions.

6. Collect: Observe during break for five days; send the form link.

7. Store: Enter the observation counts into Google Sheets; download a copy to a USB drive as backup.

8. Ethics: Ask the principal's permission; tell students the survey is anonymous and voluntary; do not record names.

Remember the steps with: "Quiet Teachers Make Students Think, Check, Save Ethically" → Question, Type, Method, Sample, Tools, Collect, Store, Ethics.

Mistake: Starting to collect data before writing a clear research question. You then collect lots of data that does not answer anything. Fix: Always write the question first. Another mistake: keeping data in only one place – if the file is lost, the project is lost. Always keep a backup.

6.7 Presenting Data Using Digital Tools: 6.7.1 Spreadsheets and 6.7.2 Presentations

Collecting data is only one part of the work. You must also present your findings so that others can understand them quickly and easily. Digital tools make this much easier. The four main tools are spreadsheets, presentations, infographics and reports.

6.7.1 Spreadsheets

Spreadsheets such as MS Excel, Google Sheets and Apple Numbers arrange information in rows (horizontal, numbered 1, 2, 3…) and columns (vertical, lettered A, B, C…). The box where a row and column meet is a cell, named by its column and row, e.g. B2. With a spreadsheet you can:

  • Enter data systematically (one record per row).
  • Calculate totals, averages and percentages.
  • Sort (arrange in order) and filter (show only some rows) records.
  • Create charts and graphs automatically.

A formula always starts with =. A range such as B2:B4 means "cells B2 to B4".

FormulaWhat it does

=MEDIAN(B2:B4)Middle value when sorted.
=COUNT(B2:B4)Counts cells containing numbers.
=COUNTA(C2:C4)Counts cells that are not empty (text or numbers).

6.7.2 Presentations

Presentation software such as PowerPoint, Google Slides and Canva helps you explain findings to an audience in an organised way. Good practice:

  • Begin with a clear title and the research question.
  • Use short bullet points instead of long paragraphs.
  • Include tables, charts and key findings.
  • Keep the design simple and readable (large font, few colours).

Spreadsheets and presentations work together: you make a chart from the spreadsheet data and insert it into the slides.

Spreadsheet data→Chart / Graph→Insert→Slides

Presentation rule of thumb: one idea per slide, and no more than about six short bullet points. If you are reading long sentences off the slide, the slide has too much text.

Mistake: Typing a result by hand (e.g. writing "3.33" in a cell) instead of using a formula. If the data changes, the typed result becomes wrong. Fix: Always use formulas like =AVERAGE(B2:B4) so results update automatically. Also remember every formula must begin with =.

6.7.3 – 6.7.4 Infographics and Reports

6.7.3 Infographics

An infographic (information + graphic) is a visual way of presenting information, facts or data so that people can understand the main idea quickly and easily. Tools such as Canva, Power BI or Tableau can be used. A good infographic should:

  • Simplify complex concepts.
  • Highlight the most important findings and results.
  • Use clear visuals, limited text and accurate information.

6.7.4 Reports

Reports are structured documents used to present collected data, findings, analysis and conclusions. Tools like MS Word or Google Docs are standard. A good report should:

  • Start with a clear title, introduction and purpose.
  • Present the collected data using tables or charts.
  • Explain findings in simple language.
  • Include conclusions and recommendations based on the data.

Optional: you may use AI tools carefully to improve wording, but always review for accuracy. AI can make mistakes or invent facts, and you are responsible for what you submit.

Visual rule of thumb: a good chart or infographic can explain complex data faster than a long paragraph. However, visuals must always be accurate and based strictly on the real data collected – never exaggerated.

The methodology is the plan of methods a researcher uses to answer the question.

Step 1: Perform advanced searches

6.8 Steps 4 – 6: Survey Preparation and Primary Data Collection ★ Golden

Step 4: Prepare the survey

Design clear, unbiased questions. Each question is linked to the type of data it produces:

QuestionType of data

Step 5: Choose the sample

The sample is the group of people who provide the data. It is usually impossible to ask everyone (the whole population), so a smaller group is chosen. The students decide to survey 40 Class XI students from different sections to get a representative mix of habits. "Representative" means the sample looks like the whole group – it includes different kinds of students, not just your friends.

Step 6: Collect primary data (ethically)

Ethics means doing what is morally right. When conducting the survey, students must follow strict ethical guidelines:

  • Ask permission before collecting responses (consent).
  • Avoid collecting sensitive personal information.
  • Do not record names unless absolutely necessary (anonymity).
  • Keep the responses private.
  • Use the data only for educational purposes.

Why "different sections" matters. Suppose the group surveyed only the 40 students of the Pre-Engineering section who sit in the front rows.

Problem: these students might study more than average, so the results would show less phone distraction than is true for the whole class.

Fix: pick students from every section (e.g. 10 each from Pre-Medical, Pre-Engineering, Computer Science and Commerce). Now the sample better represents all Class XI students.

Ethical opening text for the form: "This survey is for a class project on phone use and study habits. It is anonymous – do not write your name. Your answers will be kept private and used only for this project. Do you agree to take part? ○ Yes ○ No"

Ethics in five words: Consent, No-Sensitive, Anonymous, Private, Educational – "C-NAPE". Think: "Cover the person's nape (neck) – protect them."

Mistake: Thinking a bigger sample is always enough. A sample of 200 students all from one section is still biased. Fix: A sample must be representative, not just large.

6.8 Steps 10 – 12: Data Analysis, Conclusion and Recommendations ★ Golden

Step 10: Analyse the results

To analyse means to study the data carefully to find patterns and trends. By looking at the spreadsheet and charts, the group identifies:

  • Most students (the majority) use phones for 3–5 hours daily.
  • Half use it for educational purposes (lectures/notes).
  • A clear trend: students who use phones mostly for entertainment report much higher distraction levels than those who use them for study.

Step 11: Write the conclusion

A conclusion answers the research question using the evidence:

"The research shows mobile phones have both positive and negative effects. They provide access to learning materials. However, excessive use of social media and gaming reduces concentration. Therefore, phones should not be banned, but managed wisely."

Step 12: Recommendations

Recommendations are practical suggestions based on the data. The students recommend:

  • Turn off social media notifications while studying.
  • Set daily screen-time limits.
  • Take physical breaks from screens to protect health.

From numbers to a finding. Using the estimated chart counts (12, 21, 7 out of 40):

Step 1: 21 ÷ 40 = 52.5%. Since 52.5% > 50%, the statement "most students use phones for 3–5 hours" is supported.

Step 2: "Half use it for educational purposes" means about 20 of the 40 students chose Lectures or Notes as their main activity (20 ÷ 40 = 50%). Formula: =COUNTIF(D2:D41,"Lectures")+COUNTIF(D2:D41,"Notes").

Step 3 – Compare groups: filter the sheet to show only Social Media/Games users and count how many said "Yes" to distracted; then do the same for Lectures/Notes users. If the entertainment group has a much higher share of "Yes", that is the trend.

Step 4 – Link to secondary data: the article said excessive screen time lowers concentration; the survey agrees, so the conclusion is stronger.

Conclusion formula: "The data shows … However … Therefore …" – state the main finding, mention the other side, then give the overall answer.

Mistake: Writing a conclusion that is not based on the data, e.g. "Phones should be banned" when the data showed phones also help learning. Fix: Every sentence in the conclusion and recommendations must be supported by your findings. Also, a trend between two things (entertainment use and distraction) shows a link; be careful before claiming one definitely causes the other.

Chapter 6 Review

Chapter summary

  • Digital literacy is the ability to understand and use digital devices, the internet and online tools safely, effectively and responsibly – not just using devices, but finding, evaluating and creating information.
  • In the Information Age, access to technology is not enough; digital literacy needs technical, cognitive and social abilities.
  • Qualitative data is descriptive and non-numerical (opinions, reasons, behaviour) and explains why/how.
  • Quantitative data is numerical, countable or measurable (hours, scores, percentages) and is used for comparing and charting.
  • The five data-collection strategies are interviews, surveys, prototypes, observation and simulations.
  • Interviews are planned conversations giving mostly qualitative data; surveys ask the same questions to many people, on paper or with Google/Microsoft Forms.
  • Good survey questions are clear, short, unbiased and ask about one thing; avoid leading, double-barrelled, vague and overlapping questions.
  • A prototype is an early version tested for feedback; observation records what happens without asking questions; a simulation is a computer model used when real experiments are costly, risky or difficult.
  • Primary data is collected first-hand (high control); secondary data was collected by others and reused (limited control).
  • Designing a data-collection approach has eight steps: question, data type, method, sample, tools, collect, store with backup, and ethics (consent is mandatory).
  • Spreadsheets organise and calculate (e.g. =SUM(), =AVERAGE(), =COUNTIF()); presentations deliver to an audience; infographics give quick visual summaries; reports give detailed structure.
  • Visuals must always be accurate and based strictly on real data, never exaggerated.
  • Advanced search operators such as " ", filetype:pdf and site:.edu help find reliable sources efficiently; sources must be evaluated for author, date, reliability and bias.
  • Ethics: get consent, avoid sensitive information, keep anonymity, keep responses private, and use data only for educational purposes.
  • The digital inquiry project follows Question → Search → Collect → Analyse → Create in 12 steps.
  • A digital artefact (report, presentation, infographic or dashboard) answers the research question using evidence and visuals, following a ten-part structure ending in references.

Short questions — model answers

  1. Digital literacy is the ability to understand and use digital devices, the internet and online tools in a safe, effective and responsible manner. It involves technical, cognitive and social abilities.
  2. In the Information Age vast amounts of data are produced and shared instantly. Owning a device does not mean you can judge or use this information well. Users must develop skills to understand, manage and use information wisely, and to tell reliable information from unreliable.
  3. Qualitative data is descriptive and non-numerical; it explains why and how (e.g. students' opinions about online classes; observation notes). Quantitative data is numerical, countable or measurable; it is used for comparing and making charts (e.g. marks in a test; hours spent online).
  4. An interview is a planned conversation with one person or a small group, giving mostly qualitative, in-depth answers. A survey asks the same set of questions to many people (on paper or via Google/Microsoft Forms) and is efficient for large groups; online surveys also collect responses and make charts automatically.
  5. A prototype is a simple early version of a product, model or solution. Users try it and give feedback; this feedback is data used to improve the product before time and effort are spent on the final version.
  6. A simulation is used when real experiments are costly, risky or difficult. Example: a flight simulator to train pilots, or a traffic simulation to test the effect of closing a road.
  7. Purpose: primary data is prepared for a specific study; secondary data is reused for a new purpose. Control: the researcher has high control over primary data (questions and process) and limited control over secondary data because it already exists.
  8. Any four: ask permission (consent); avoid sensitive personal information; do not record names unless necessary (anonymity); keep responses private; use data only for educational purposes.
  9. Quotes " " find an exact phrase, e.g. "study habits" students. filetype: finds a file format, e.g. "academic performance" filetype:pdf gives only PDFs. site: limits to a type of website, e.g. digital distraction site:.edu searches only educational sites.
  10. A digital artefact is a final product created with digital technology that answers the research question with evidence and conclusions. Options: digital report (MS Word/Google Docs), presentation (PowerPoint/Canva/Slides), infographic (Canva/Adobe Express), dashboard (Power BI/Tableau).

Long questions — model answers

  1. Eight steps applied to water wastage. (1) Define the question: "How much water is wasted at the college taps during break time, and why?" (2) Data type: both – quantitative (number of taps left running, minutes running) and qualitative (students' reasons). (3) Methods: observation to count running taps; a short survey to learn habits and reasons. (4) Sample: all 12 taps observed over 5 break times; 30 students from different classes surveyed. (5) Tools: an observation sheet (Tap no., Time, Left running Y/N, Minutes) and a Google Form. (6) Collect carefully, at the same time each day, recording accurately. (7) Store data in Google Sheets and keep a backup copy (download or USB). (8) Ethics: get the principal's permission; the survey is voluntary and anonymous with a consent question; data used only for the project.
  2. Survey on travel to college (model). Consent: "This anonymous survey is for a Class XI project on travel to college. Do not write your name. Answers will be kept private and used only for this project. Do you agree to take part? Yes / No." Questions: Q1 How do you usually travel to college? Walk / Bus / Rickshaw / Motorbike / Car / Other – quantitative (categories). Q2 How many minutes does your journey usually take? ___ – quantitative. Q3 How many days a week are you late because of travel? 0 / 1 / 2 / 3 or more – quantitative. Q4 "My journey to college is safe." 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree) – quantitative (rating). Q5 Do you travel alone? Yes / No – quantitative (count). Q6 What is the biggest problem you face while travelling? – qualitative. Q7 Suggest one way the college could make travel easier. – qualitative. Questions are unbiased, ask one thing each, and options do not overlap. Sample: about 40 Class XI students, roughly 10 from each section, to be representative. Ethics: consent, no sensitive information (no home address), no names (anonymity), private storage, educational use only. Data is collected through Google Forms and exported to Google Sheets for analysis.
  3. Spreadsheet problem. (a) =SUM(B2:B11) → 2+4+3+5+6+4+3+4+1+4 = 36. =AVERAGE(B2:B11) → 36 ÷ 10 = 3.6 hours. Sorted: 1, 2, 3, 3, 4, 4, 4, 4, 5, 6; =MEDIAN(B2:B11) → (4 + 4) ÷ 2 = 4. =MODE(B2:B11) → 4 (appears 4 times). =MAX(B2:B11) → 6; =MIN(B2:B11) → 1. (b) =COUNTIF(B2:B11,">4") → values 5 and 6 → 2 students; percentage =COUNTIF(B2:B11,">4")/COUNT(B2:B11) → 2 ÷ 10 = 20%. (c) 1–2 hrs: 2 students (2, 1); 3–4 hrs: 6 students (4, 3, 4, 3, 4, 4); 5+ hrs: 2 students (5, 6). Check: 2 + 6 + 2 = 10 ✓. A bar/column chart is suitable for comparing the groups (or a pie chart to show 20%, 60%, 20%).
  4. Twelve steps. (1) Advanced searches with operators such as "mobile phone use" "study habits" students, "academic performance" filetype:pdf, digital distraction site:.edu; evaluate sources (author, recent, reliable site, facts/bias). (2) Identify data types: quantitative (hours daily, % distracted) and qualitative (reasons, suggestions). (3) Select method: survey via Google Forms/paper, because the same questions go to many students. (4) Prepare the survey: hours daily (quantitative), main activity (quantitative categories), how the phone helps (qualitative), one responsible-use suggestion (qualitative). (5) Sample: 40 Class XI students from different sections. (6) Collect ethically: consent, no sensitive data, anonymity, privacy, educational use only. (7) Secondary data: an article saying excessive screen time lowers concentration, to compare with results. (8) Organise in Excel/Google Sheets (Student, Hours, Study Use, Main Activity, Distracted?), sort, total, average. (9) Charts: bar chart of daily phone use (1–3, 3–5, 5+ hrs). (10) Analyse: majority use 3–5 hours; half use for education; entertainment users report more distraction. (11) Conclusion: phones have positive and negative effects; should be managed wisely, not banned. (12) Recommendations: turn off social media notifications, set screen-time limits, take physical breaks. Artefact: one of report, presentation, infographic or dashboard, structured as Title, Research Question, Introduction, Method, Primary Findings, Secondary Summary, Charts, Conclusion, Recommendations, References.
  5. AI tools for homework (model). Survey: consent statement (anonymous, voluntary, educational use only). Q1 Have you used an AI tool (e.g. ChatGPT, Gemini) for homework? Yes / No – quantitative. Q2 How many times a week? 0 / 1–2 / 3–5 / 6 or more – quantitative (non-overlapping categories). Q3 What do you use it for most? Explaining topics / Checking answers / Writing assignments / Translation / Other – quantitative (categories). Q4 "AI tools help me understand my subjects better." 1–5 rating – quantitative. Q5 Do you check AI answers against your textbook? Always / Sometimes / Never – quantitative. Q6 Describe one problem you have faced with AI answers. – qualitative. Sample: about 40 Class XI students from all sections. Secondary sources: "generative AI" students homework filetype:pdf; "AI in education" site:.edu; "artificial intelligence" policy site:.gov.pk; evaluate each for author, date, reliability and bias. Artefact: a digital report (MS Word/Google Docs) because the principal needs the full study – method, findings, charts, conclusion, recommendations and references – in a structured document; a one-page infographic could be attached as a quick summary. Charts would be made in Google Sheets from the form responses, and every visual must match the real data.

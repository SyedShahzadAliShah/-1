1.1.1 Discrete and Continuous Quantities

Everything around you that can be measured or counted is a quantity. Quantities come in two kinds.

Discrete quantities are separate, countable items with specific values. You can count them one by one: 1, 2, 3 … There is nothing "in between". You cannot have 2.5 students in a classroom.

Continuous quantities can take any value within a range. They flow smoothly without breaks. Water can be 30 °C, 30.5 °C, 30.51 °C, and so on — there is always another value in between.

Discrete (counted)Continuous (measured)
Number of students in a classroomTemperature of water
Number of cars in a parking lotSpeed of a moving car
Has fixed, separate valuesHas any value in a range

Stairs vs Ramp. On stairs you can only stand on step 1, 2 or 3 — that is discrete data. On a ramp you can stand at any height — that is continuous data. Ask yourself: "Do I count it or measure it?" Count → discrete. Measure → continuous.

Students sometimes say "money is continuous because amounts are large". No — rupees and paisa are counted in fixed units, so money is discrete. Size does not matter; what matters is whether values in between are possible.

1.1.2 Introduction to Digital Systems

A digital system is an electronic system that uses only two values: 0 and 1. These two values are called binary digits, or bits for short.

  • 0 represents OFF, LOW, or FALSE.
  • 1 represents ON, HIGH, or TRUE.

Why only two values? Because it is easy for an electronic circuit to tell "voltage present" from "no voltage". Every number, letter, photo and song in a computer is finally stored as a long pattern of these bits.

Advantages of digital systems

AdvantageDetail
ReliableLess affected by noise and interference.
AccurateNo loss of quality when copying data.
Easy to designOnly two states (0 and 1) to handle.
ProgrammableCan be easily controlled by software.

Examples of digital systems

  • Computers: process data as 0s and 1s.
  • Smartphones: store photos and music as binary data.
  • Digital cameras: capture images as binary code.

Why is copying digital data "accurate"?

Step 1: A song is stored as bits, for example 10110010…

Step 2: When you copy it to a USB, the computer copies each bit exactly: 1 stays 1, 0 stays 0.

Step 3: Even after 100 copies the bits are identical, so the song sounds the same. An old cassette tape (analog) loses a little quality every time it is copied.

Think of a bit as a light switch: it is either ON (1) or OFF (0). A digital system is a huge panel of such switches.

2. Give two advantages of digital systems.

Answers: 1. ON, HIGH or TRUE. 2. Any two of: reliable (less affected by noise), accurate (no loss when copying), easy to design (two states), programmable (controlled by software).

1.1.3 Analog and Digital Signals ★ Golden

A signal is a changing voltage (or other quantity) that carries information.

Analog signals are continuous signals that change smoothly over time, like a sine wave. Examples: the human voice, a traditional mercury thermometer.

Digital signals have only two levels: HIGH (1) and LOW (0). They jump instantly between these values, which creates a square wave. Example: computer data.

FeatureAnalog dataDigital data
NatureContinuous flow.Discrete, separate values (bits).
Wave shapeSine wave (smooth curve).Square wave (sudden jumps).
ReliabilityLow (affected by noise).High (resistant to noise).
ExamplesHuman voice, traditional thermometer.Computer data.

Why is digital more resistant to noise?

Step 1: Suppose a wire carries 5 V for HIGH and 0 V for LOW.

Step 2: Noise adds a small error, so 5 V arrives as 4.6 V and 0 V arrives as 0.3 V.

Step 3: The receiver only asks "Is it closer to HIGH or LOW?" 4.6 V is still read as 1 and 0.3 V is still read as 0. The data is perfect.

Step 4: In an analog signal, every exact value carries meaning, so 4.6 V instead of 5 V really changes the sound or reading. The noise cannot be removed.

Analog = A smooth curve. Digital = Doorsteps. Analog looks like ocean waves; digital looks like a castle wall (square teeth).

Do not draw a digital signal with sloping sides. A digital signal jumps instantly, so its edges are straight vertical lines. Also do not confuse "digital" with "electronic": an analog signal can also be electronic.

اینالاگ سگنل وقت کے ساتھ ہموار انداز میں بدلتا ہے، جیسے سائن ویو (Sine Wave)؛ انسانی آواز اس کی مثال ہے۔ ڈیجیٹل سگنل کی صرف دو سطحیں ہوتی ہیں، HIGH (1) اور LOW (0)، اور یہ اچانک ایک سے دوسری پر جاتا ہے، اس لیے مربع لہر (Square Wave) بنتی ہے۔ ڈیجیٹل سگنل شور سے کم متاثر ہوتا ہے، اس لیے زیادہ قابلِ بھروسا ہے۔

1.1.4 Boolean Algebra

Boolean algebra was developed by the English mathematician George Boole. It is a kind of algebra that deals with only two values: TRUE (1) and FALSE (0). It is the foundation of digital electronics and computer programming — every if condition in a program is a small piece of Boolean algebra.

ConceptDescriptionExample
VariablesLetters that hold the value 0 or 1.A, B, C, X, Y
OperationsActions performed on variables.AND, OR, NOT
ExpressionsCombinations of variables and operations.A AND B, Y = A + B

Some simple rules (laws) follow directly from the meaning of AND, OR and NOT. They are useful when you check or simplify answers:

OR rulesAND rulesNOT rules
A + 0 = AA · 1 = A(A')' = A
A + 1 = 1A · 0 = 0A + A' = 1
A + A = AA · A = AA · A' = 0

Using a law: Simplify Y = A'B + AB.

Step 1: Both terms contain B, so take B out: Y = B(A' + A).

Step 2: A' + A = 1 (a variable OR its opposite is always 1).

Step 3: Y = B · 1 = B.

In Boolean algebra "+" means OR, not addition. So 1 + 1 = 1 (TRUE OR TRUE is TRUE), not 2. There is no value bigger than 1.

بولین الجبرا جارج بول (George Boole) نے بنایا۔ اس میں صرف دو قیمتیں ہوتی ہیں: درست (1) اور غلط (0)۔ متغیرات (Variables) جیسے A اور B صرف 0 یا 1 رکھتے ہیں، آپریشنز AND، OR اور NOT ہیں، اور ان کے ملاپ سے ایکسپریشن (Expression) بنتا ہے۔

1. Who developed Boolean algebra?

2. In Boolean algebra, what is 1 + 1?

Answers: 1. George Boole. 2. 1 (because + means OR).

1.1.5 Boolean Operations

There are three basic Boolean operations. Every other operation can be made from them.

1. AND operation ( · ) — gives output 1 only when ALL inputs are 1. Expression: Y = A · B (often written just AB).

2. OR operation ( + ) — gives output 1 when ANY input is 1. Expression: Y = A + B.

3. NOT operation ( ' or ¯ ) — inverts (flips) the input. If the input is 1 the output is 0, and if the input is 0 the output is 1. It is a unary operation, meaning it works on only one operand. Expression: Y = A' (also written as A with a bar on top, A̅).

OperationReal-life analogy
ANDA door with two locks. The door opens only when both locks are unlocked.
ORA room with two doors. You can enter if either door is open.
NOTA room light switch. When the switch is ON the room is lit; when it is OFF the room is dark — the state is the opposite of "dark".

Evaluate each expression for A = 1, B = 0.

(a) A · B = 1 · 0 = 0 (not all inputs are 1).

(b) A + B = 1 + 0 = 1 (at least one input is 1).

(c) B' = 0' = 1 (NOT flips 0 to 1).

(d) A · B' = 1 · 1 = 1.

AND is "strict" — everyone must agree (all 1s). OR is "easy-going" — one 1 is enough. NOT is "the rebel" — it always says the opposite.

AND آپریشن تب ہی 1 دیتا ہے جب تمام ان پٹ 1 ہوں (دو تالوں والا دروازہ)۔ OR آپریشن تب 1 دیتا ہے جب کوئی ایک ان پٹ بھی 1 ہو (دو دروازوں والا کمرہ)۔ NOT آپریشن ان پٹ کو الٹ دیتا ہے اور صرف ایک ان پٹ پر کام کرتا ہے، اس لیے اسے یونری (Unary) کہتے ہیں۔

1. What symbol is used for the OR operation?

2. Why is NOT called a unary operation?

3. Find A + B' when A = 0, B = 1.

Answers: 1. "+". 2. Because it works on only one operand (one input). 3. B' = 0, so 0 + 0 = 0.

A school bell circuit. The bell (Y) should ring when the timer says "period over" (A = 1) and the school is open (B = 1).

1.1.7 Universal Logic Gates (NAND and NOR)

NAND means "NOT AND": it is an AND gate followed by a NOT. NOR means "NOT OR": an OR gate followed by a NOT. On the symbol, the NOT is shown as a small bubble at the output.

NAND and NOR are called universal gates because any logic circuit in the world can be built using only NAND gates (or only NOR gates). Factories like this: they can mass-produce one type of chip and build everything from it.

Show that a NAND gate can work as a NOT gate.

Step 1: Connect the same input A to both inputs of the NAND gate. Then Y = (A · A)'.

Step 2: Since A · A = A, we get Y = A'.

Step 3: Check: A = 0 → (0·0)' = 0' = 1. A = 1 → (1·1)' = 1' = 0. This is exactly the NOT truth table.

In the same way, a NOR gate with both inputs tied together gives (A + A)' = A'. And a NAND followed by a NAND-as-NOT gives ((A·B)')' = A·B, an AND gate. This is why they are "universal".

NAND is the exact opposite of AND (0 only at 11). NOR is the exact opposite of OR (1 only at 00). Just flip the AND/OR output column.

Do not forget the bubble. Without the small circle, a NAND symbol becomes an AND symbol and your answer is wrong. Also, (A · B)' is not the same as A' · B': for A = 1, B = 0, (1·0)' = 1 but 0·1 = 0.

Minterms (SOP) and Maxterms (POS)

Sometimes you are given a truth table and must find the expression. There are two standard ways.

Minterm (used in SOP — Sum of Products): a product (AND) of all the variables, written for a row whose output is 1. A variable is complemented (A') if its value is 0, and uncomplemented (A) if its value is 1. OR-ing all the minterms gives the SOP expression.

Maxterm (used in POS — Product of Sums): a sum (OR) of all the variables, written for a row whose output is 0. A variable is complemented (A') if its value is 1, and uncomplemented (A) if its value is 0. AND-ing all the maxterms gives the POS expression.

3. Write the maxterm for A = 0, B = 1.

Answers: 1. AND. 2. AB'C. 3. A + B'.

1.1.11 Logic Diagrams ★ Golden

A logic diagram shows how logic gates are connected to create a circuit. It is a "picture" of a Boolean expression. To draw one, use the order of precedence: the operation with the highest priority is drawn first (nearest the inputs), and the last operation is the gate that gives the final output.

Example 1: Draw the logic diagram for Y = A · B + C.

Step 1: Find the operations: AND ( · ) and OR ( + ).

Step 2: AND has higher precedence than OR, so draw the AND gate first, with inputs A and B. Its output is A · B.

Step 3: Draw an OR gate. Feed it A · B and C. Its output is Y.

Example 2: Draw the logic diagram for Y = (A + B) · C'.

Step 1: Parentheses have the highest priority, so draw the OR gate for A + B first.

Step 2: NOT is next: draw a NOT gate on input C to get C'.

Step 3: The last operation is AND: feed (A + B) and C' into an AND gate to get Y.

You can check this circuit against the truth table in 1.1.8 (worked example 2): Y = 1 only for rows 010, 100 and 110.

Read the expression and ask "What is done last?" That gate goes on the far right, next to Y. Work backwards to the inputs. Count gates: Y = A·B + C needs 2 gates; Y = (A+B)·C' needs 3.

Do not draw a separate wire for a variable each time it appears and then leave one unconnected. Every gate input must be joined to an input variable or to another gate's output, and every wire should be clearly labelled in exams.

لاجک ڈایاگرام دکھاتا ہے کہ گیٹس آپس میں کیسے جڑ کر سرکٹ بناتے ہیں۔ ترجیحی ترتیب کے مطابق جو آپریشن پہلے ہوتا ہے اس کا گیٹ پہلے (ان پٹ کے قریب) بنایا جاتا ہے، اور آخری آپریشن کا گیٹ آؤٹ پٹ Y دیتا ہے۔ مثلاً Y = A·B + C میں پہلے AND گیٹ اور پھر OR گیٹ بنتا ہے۔

Typical question: "Draw the logic diagram of Y = …" and sometimes "also make its truth table" (4–5 marks). Write the precedence steps in one line, draw neat gate symbols with labelled intermediate outputs (like A·B), and finish with the truth table. Marks are lost for wrong gate shapes and missing bubbles.

1. In Y = A + B · C, which gate is drawn first?

2. How many gates are needed for Y = A' · B?

Answers: 1. The AND gate (for B · C). 2. Two: one NOT gate (for A') and one AND gate.

1.1.13 Karnaugh Maps (K-Maps) ★ Golden

A Karnaugh map (K-map) is a graphical tool used to simplify Boolean expressions. It is a grid of cells — one cell for each row of the truth table. The cells are arranged so that neighbouring (adjacent) cells differ by only ONE bit. This ordering is called Gray code.

You write the output (1 or 0) of each row into its cell, then group the 1s in rectangles of size 1, 2, 4 or 8 (powers of 2). Each group becomes one simple term. This simplifies the equation without long algebra.

Two-variable K-map (A, B)

2 variables need 22 = 4 cells.

B = 0B = 1
A = 0Cell 0 (A'B')Cell 1 (A'B)
A = 1Cell 2 (AB')Cell 3 (AB)

Three-variable K-map (A, B, C)

3 variables need 23 = 8 cells. The columns follow the Gray code sequence 00, 01, 11, 10 (not 00, 01, 10, 11). This makes adjacent cells differ by only one variable.

BC = 00BC = 01BC = 11BC = 10
A = 0m₀ (A'B'C')m₁ (A'B'C)m₃ (A'BC)m₂ (A'BC')
A = 1m₄ (AB'C')m₅ (AB'C)m₇ (ABC)m₆ (ABC')

Grouping rules

  1. Group only 1s. Groups must be rectangles of size 1, 2, 4 or 8 — never 3, 5 or 6.
  2. Make groups as large as possible. Bigger groups give simpler terms.
  3. Use as few groups as possible, but every 1 must be in at least one group.
  4. Overlapping is allowed — a 1 may be used in more than one group.
  5. Wrapping around the edges is allowed: a cell in the far-left column is adjacent to the cell in the far-right column (BC = 00 is next to BC = 10).
  6. To read a group: variables that change (0 to 1) inside the group are eliminated. Variables that stay the same are kept — plain if they stay 1, complemented if they stay 0.

Group size tells you how many variables disappear: in a 3-variable map, a group of 2 leaves 2 variables, a group of 4 leaves 1 variable, and a group of 8 means Y = 1.

K-map example 1 (2 variables): simplify Y = A'B + AB.

Step 1: The minterms are A'B (cell 1) and AB (cell 3). Put 1 in those cells, 0 elsewhere.

B = 0B = 1
A = 001
A = 101

Step 2: The two 1s are in the same column, one above the other. Make one group of 2.

Step 3: Inside the group, A changes (0 then 1) → eliminate A. B stays 1 → keep B.

Result: Y = B. This agrees with the algebra in 1.1.4 and the minterm table in 1.1.8.

K-map example 2 (2 variables, overlapping): simplify Y = A'B' + A'B + AB.

Step 1: Put 1s in cells 0, 1 and 3.

B = 0B = 1
A = 011
A = 101

Step 2: Group 1 = the top row (cells 0 and 1). A stays 0, B changes → term A'.

Step 3: Group 2 = the right column (cells 1 and 3). A changes, B stays 1 → term B. Cell 1 is used twice — overlapping is allowed.

Result: Y = A' + B. Check: A' + B is 0 only when A = 1 and B = 0, which is exactly the one 0 cell (cell 2). ✓

K-map example 3 (3 variables): simplify the majority function Y = Σm(3, 5, 6, 7) = A'BC + AB'C + ABC' + ABC.

Step 1: Fill the map. Careful — the columns are in Gray order 00, 01, 11, 10, so m₃ is in the third column and m₂ in the fourth.

BC = 00BC = 01BC = 11BC = 10
A = 0001 (m₃)0
A = 101 (m₅)1 (m₇)1 (m₆)

Step 2: No group of 4 is possible (there is no 2×2 block or full row of 1s). So make groups of 2, each using m₇:

  • Group 1: m₃ + m₇ (column BC = 11). A changes; B = 1, C = 1 stay → BC.
  • Group 2: m₅ + m₇ (row A = 1, columns 01 and 11). B changes; A = 1, C = 1 stay → AC.
  • Group 3: m₇ + m₆ (row A = 1, columns 11 and 10). C changes; A = 1, B = 1 stay → AB.

Result: Y = AB + BC + AC. Four 3-variable terms (needing 4 three-input AND gates) became three 2-variable terms.

Check one row: A=1, B=0, C=1 → AB = 0, BC = 0, AC = 1 → Y = 1. In the truth table, row 5 is 1. ✓

K-map example 4 (3 variables, wrap-around): simplify Y = Σm(0, 2, 4, 6).

BC = 00BC = 01BC = 11BC = 10
A = 01001
A = 11001

Step 1: The 1s are in the far-left column (BC = 00) and the far-right column (BC = 10).

Step 2: Because the map wraps around, these two columns are adjacent. Make one group of 4: m₀, m₄, m₂, m₆.

Step 3: In this group A changes (0, 1) and B changes (0, 1). Only C stays the same: C = 0 → term C'.

Result: Y = C'. Without wrapping you would get two groups (B'C' + BC'), which is correct but not the simplest.

K-map example 5 (3 variables): simplify Y = Σm(1, 4, 5, 6, 7) — this is the output of Y = A + B'C from 1.1.8.

BC = 00BC = 01BC = 11BC = 10
A = 00100
A = 11111

Step 1: Group of 4: the whole row A = 1 (m₄, m₅, m₇, m₆). B and C both change; A stays 1 → A.

Step 2: m₁ is still alone. Pair it with m₅ below it (column BC = 01). A changes; B = 0, C = 1 stay → B'C.

Result: Y = A + B'C — the K-map confirms the original expression was already in its simplest form.

Going further — a 4-variable K-map: simplify Y = Σm(0, 2, 5, 7, 8, 10, 13, 15). (Not required by the slides, but a good test of the same rules.) 4 variables need 16 cells; both rows (AB) and columns (CD) use Gray order 00, 01, 11, 10.

CD = 00CD = 01CD = 11CD = 10
AB = 001 (m₀)001 (m₂)
AB = 0101 (m₅)1 (m₇)0
AB = 1101 (m₁₃)1 (m₁₅)0
AB = 101 (m₈)001 (m₁₀)

Step 1: The centre 2×2 block (m₅, m₇, m₁₃, m₁₅) is a group of 4. A and C change; B = 1 and D = 1 stay → BD.

Step 2: The four corners (m₀, m₂, m₈, m₁₀) are adjacent through wrap-around (left–right and top–bottom). A and C change; B = 0 and D = 0 stay → B'D'.

Result: Y = BD + B'D' (this is B XNOR D). Eight 4-variable minterms became two 2-variable terms.

Gray code song: "zero-zero, zero-one, one-one, one-zero" — only one bit flips at each step. Imagine the K-map is drawn on a tube, so the left and right edges touch. For reading groups: "If it changes, it goes."

Most common K-map mistakes: (1) writing the columns as 00, 01, 10, 11 — this puts m₂ and m₃ in the wrong places; (2) making groups of 3 or 6 — only 1, 2, 4, 8 are allowed; (3) grouping diagonally — groups must be rectangles of side-by-side cells; (4) stopping at small groups when a bigger group (or a wrap-around group) is possible.

کارنو میپ (K-Map) بولین ایکسپریشن کو آسان بنانے کا تصویری طریقہ ہے۔ اس کے ساتھ والے خانوں میں صرف ایک بِٹ کا فرق ہوتا ہے (گرے کوڈ: 00، 01، 11، 10)۔ 1 والے خانوں کو 1، 2، 4 یا 8 کے گروپوں میں جتنا بڑا ممکن ہو جوڑیں؛ گروپ ایک دوسرے پر چڑھ سکتے ہیں اور کناروں سے گھوم کر بھی بن سکتے ہیں۔ گروپ کے اندر جو متغیر بدلتا ہے وہ ختم ہو جاتا ہے۔

K-maps are a favourite long question (5–6 marks): "Simplify F(A,B,C) = Σm(…) using a K-map." Draw the map with labelled Gray-code headings, fill every cell (write the 0s too), circle the groups clearly, write the term for each group beside it, and give the final simplified expression. Examiners often add "draw the logic diagram of the result" — so practise drawing it too.

1. In a 3-variable K-map, which column is next to BC = 01 on the right?

2. Simplify Y = Σm(2, 3) for two variables A, B.

3. Simplify Y = Σm(4, 5, 6, 7) for three variables.

Answers: 1. BC = 11. 2. Cells 2 and 3 are the row A = 1; B changes → Y = A. 3. The whole row A = 1 is a group of 4 → Y = A.

1.2.1 – 1.2.3 Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC) ★ Golden

The Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC) is a structured process that guides software development from beginning to end. It ensures clear goals, better planning, risk reduction and high quality. Without it, teams forget features, go over budget and deliver buggy software.

SDLC has 6 phases:

1. Requirement Analysis→2. System Design→3. Implementation→4. Testing→5. Deployment→6. Maintenance

Phases 1–3 in detail

PhaseActivities and detailsDeliverable (what it produces)
1. Requirement AnalysisMeet clients, understand their needs, list the features. This is the most important phase — mistakes here spread to every later phase.SRS Document (Software Requirements Specification)
2. System DesignCreate the High-Level Design (overall architecture), the Low-Level Design (database, UI screens) and flowcharts.Design Document
3. ImplementationWrite the actual code in programming languages (Python, Java, etc.) based on the design documents.Working Source Code

Phases 4–6 in detail

PhaseActivities and detailsDeliverable
4. TestingFind and fix bugs (errors), using Black Box and White Box testing.Test Reports, bug-free software
5. DeploymentRelease the software to end-users: Direct, Phased, or Pilot release.Live Software
6. MaintenanceProvide support and updates, and fix post-release issues.Patches, Updated Versions

Deployment methods: In a direct release the old system is switched off and the new one switched on at once. In a phased release the new system is introduced part by part. In a pilot release one small group (for example one branch or one class) uses it first before everyone else.

Testing methods

Black Box Testing

  • The tester does not see the code.
  • Tests functionality: give inputs, check outputs.
  • Done by dedicated testers.

White Box Testing

  • The tester can see the code.
  • Tests the internal logic and structure.
  • Done by developers.

Building a house = SDLC.

Phase 1 (Requirements): you ask the family what rooms they need — 3 bedrooms, a kitchen, a garage.

Phase 2 (Design): the architect draws the blueprints.

Phase 3 (Implementation): the builders lay the bricks.

Phase 4 (Testing): an inspector checks the wiring and the plumbing.

Phase 5 (Deployment): the family moves in.

Phase 6 (Maintenance): repairs, painting and a new room added later.

Black box vs white box on a login screen. A black-box tester types a correct password and a wrong password and checks that the first is accepted and the second rejected — without looking at the code. A white-box tester (the developer) reads the code and checks that every if branch, for example the "account locked after 3 tries" branch, actually runs correctly.

Remember the order with "Real Developers Implement, Test, Deploy, Maintain" (R-D-I-T-D-M). Black box = a closed box (you can't see inside). White box = a glass box (you can see inside).

Do not say the SDLC ends at deployment. Maintenance is a full phase and often lasts the longest. Also, the SRS is the deliverable of Requirement Analysis, not of Design.

"Explain the phases of SDLC" is a very common long question (5–8 marks). Name all 6 phases in order, write 1–2 lines of activities for each, and state the deliverable of each (SRS, Design Document, Source Code, Test Reports, Live Software, Patches). A short question often asks you to differentiate black box and white box testing — answer in a 3-row table (sees code?, what is tested?, who does it?).

1. Which phase produces the SRS document?

2. Who usually performs white box testing?

1.2.4 The Waterfall Model ★ Golden

The Waterfall model is a linear, sequential approach to the SDLC. Each phase must be completed before the next one begins, like water falling down steps. There is no easy way to go back to a previous phase.

When to use it

  • Requirements are clear, fixed and well understood.
  • The project is small to medium in size.
  • The technology is stable (not new or experimental).
Advantages (Pros)Disadvantages (Cons)
Simple to understand and manage.Not flexible to changes.
Well documented at every step.Testing happens very late in the process.

Case study: Al-Noor Library Management System. The textbook uses this system as a Waterfall case study because the requirements were 100% fixed before coding began.

Step 1: The librarian listed exactly what was needed: add books, issue books, return books, calculate fines. Nothing would change.

Step 2: Because the list was fixed, the team could finish the full design, then all the coding, then all the testing, in order.

Step 3: Every phase produced a document, so the library has a complete record for future maintenance.

Waterfall = water only flows downwards. Once water has fallen it cannot climb back up — once a phase is finished you do not go back.

Do not choose Waterfall for a project where the customer is still unsure what they want. A late change means going back through design, coding and testing, which is slow and costly.

واٹرفال (آبشار) ماڈل ایک سیدھا اور ترتیب وار طریقہ ہے؛ ہر مرحلہ مکمل ہونے کے بعد ہی اگلا شروع ہوتا ہے اور پیچھے جانا مشکل ہوتا ہے۔ یہ تب بہتر ہے جب ضروریات واضح اور طے شدہ ہوں، جیسے النور لائبریری مینجمنٹ سسٹم میں۔ یہ سمجھنے میں آسان ہے مگر تبدیلیوں کے لیے لچکدار نہیں اور ٹیسٹنگ دیر سے ہوتی ہے۔

Expect "Explain the Waterfall model with its advantages and disadvantages" or "When should the Waterfall model be used?" Draw the step diagram, list the three "when to use" conditions, and give at least two pros and two cons. Mention the Al-Noor Library case study as your example.

1. Why is the Waterfall model called "sequential"?

2. Why did the Al-Noor Library system use Waterfall?

Answers: 1. Because each phase must be completed before the next begins, in a fixed order. 2. Because its requirements were 100% fixed before coding began.

1.2.4 The Agile Model ★ Golden

The Agile model is an iterative approach: the software is built in small, repeated cycles called sprints (usually 2–4 weeks long). Each sprint plans, builds and tests a small part of the software, and then shows it to the customer. Customer feedback is gathered continuously and used in the next sprint.

Plan→Design→Build→Test→Review & feedback→Next sprint ↺

When to use it

  • Requirements are unclear or may change.
  • The customer wants frequent progress updates (early delivery).
  • The project is complex or innovative.
Advantages (Pros)Disadvantages (Cons)
Highly flexible to changes.Less documentation.
Early delivery of working software.Unpredictable end-time and cost.

Case study: Al-Noor Student Mobile Application. This app used Agile because teachers, parents and students constantly requested new features during development.

Sprint 1 (2 weeks): build login and the timetable screen. Show it to users.

Feedback: parents ask, "Can we see attendance too?"

Sprint 2: add attendance. Teachers now ask for homework notifications.

Sprint 3: add notifications. Each sprint delivers a working app, and new ideas are welcomed instead of causing a restart.

Agile = agile athlete: quick and able to change direction. Think of making a pizza where the customer tastes each slice and you adjust the next one.

"Less documentation" does not mean "no documentation" or "no planning". Agile teams still plan every sprint; they simply value working software over long documents.

ایجائل (Agile) ماڈل میں سافٹ ویئر چھوٹے چھوٹے ادوار میں بنتا ہے جنہیں اسپرنٹ (Sprint) کہتے ہیں، جو عموماً دو سے چار ہفتے کے ہوتے ہیں۔ ہر اسپرنٹ کے بعد گاہک کی رائے لی جاتی ہے۔ یہ تبدیلیوں کے لیے بہت لچکدار ہے اور جلد کام کرنے والا سافٹ ویئر دیتا ہے، مگر اس میں دستاویزات کم ہوتی ہیں اور وقت و لاگت کا اندازہ مشکل ہوتا ہے۔ النور اسٹوڈنٹ موبائل ایپ اس کی مثال ہے۔

Questions ask you to define Agile, explain sprints, list pros/cons, or say when to use it. Always mention the words "iterative", "sprints (2–4 weeks)" and "continuous customer feedback". Use the Al-Noor Student Mobile App as your case study.

1. What is a sprint and how long does it usually last?

2. Give one disadvantage of Agile.

Answers: 1. A short development cycle that delivers a working part of the software; usually 2–4 weeks. 2. Less documentation, or unpredictable end-time and cost.

1.2.5 – 1.2.7 Waterfall vs Agile: Comparison and Case Studies

Both models follow the same SDLC phases. The difference is how they move through them: Waterfall goes through each phase once, in a straight line; Agile goes through all phases many times, in small loops.

DimensionWaterfall modelAgile model
FlexibilityRigid. Hard to make changes later.Very flexible. Adapts to changes easily in the next sprint.
Customer involvementOnly at the start (requirements) and the end (delivery).Continuous throughout the project (after every sprint).
Delivery of softwareSingle delivery at the very end.Frequent, partial deliveries after every sprint.
TestingDone late, in a dedicated testing phase.Continuous testing within every sprint.
DocumentationHeavy documentation required.Light documentation. Focus is on working software.

Choosing a model — think like the textbook.

Case A: Al-Noor Library Management System. Requirements fixed 100% before coding → no changes expected → Waterfall.

Case B: Al-Noor Student Mobile Application. Teachers, parents and students keep asking for features → requirements change → Agile.

Practice case: A bank wants a salary-calculation program for a fixed government formula that will not change. Requirements are clear and fixed, the project is medium-sized, and the technology is stable → Waterfall.

Practice case: A start-up wants a new food-delivery app and is not sure which features customers will like. Requirements are unclear and the project is innovative → Agile.

One question decides it: "Will the requirements change?" No → Waterfall. Yes / not sure → Agile.

1.3.1 – 1.3.4 Communication Models and the OSI 7-Layer Model ★ Golden

A communication model explains how data travels from one device to another through a network. Because sending data is complicated, the job is split into layers; each layer does one part of the job and passes the data to the next layer.

The OSI (Open Systems Interconnection) model was developed by ISO (International Organization for Standardization) in 1984. It divides communication into 7 layers. When you send data, it goes down the layers on your device (7 → 1), travels as bits over the cable or air, and goes up the layers on the receiving device (1 → 7).

Mnemonic (bottom to top, 1 → 7): "Please Do Not Throw Sausage Pizza Away" = Physical, Data Link, Network, Transport, Session, Presentation, Application. (Top to bottom you can use "All People Seem To Need Data Processing".)

5. SessionOpens, maintains and closes communication sessions.NetBIOS, RPC

Tracing an email through the OSI layers (sender side).

Layer 7 (Application): you press Send; your email program uses SMTP.

Layer 6 (Presentation): the text is encoded (ASCII) and may be encrypted with TLS.

Layer 5 (Session): a session is opened with the mail server.

Layer 4 (Transport): TCP breaks the data into segments and numbers them, so nothing is lost (email needs accuracy).

Layer 3 (Network): each packet gets the IP address of the destination; routers choose the path.

Layer 2 (Data Link): packets are put in frames with MAC addresses; a switch forwards them on the local network.

Layer 1 (Physical): the frames are sent as raw bits over the cable or Wi-Fi radio.

At the receiver, the same steps happen in reverse order (1 → 7).

Do not mix up the two addresses: IP address = logical address = Network layer (3); MAC address = physical address = Data Link layer (2). Also, routers work at layer 3, switches at layer 2 and hubs at layer 1.

1.3.5 – 1.3.6 TCP/IP Model vs OSI Model

The OSI model is mainly a reference model used for teaching and design. The TCP/IP model is the practical model used on the real Internet. It condenses the 7 OSI layers into just 4 layers.

3. InternetNetwork (3)

Where does each protocol/device go in TCP/IP?

HTTP (OSI layer 7) → TCP/IP Application.

SSL/TLS (OSI layer 6) → TCP/IP Application (layers 7, 6, 5 are merged).

UDP (OSI layer 4) → TCP/IP Transport.

IP and routers (OSI layer 3) → TCP/IP Internet.

Ethernet, switches, cables (OSI layers 2 and 1) → TCP/IP Network Access.

Count it as 3-1-1-2: the TCP/IP Application layer takes 3 OSI layers, Transport takes 1, Internet takes 1, Network Access takes 2. Total 3+1+1+2 = 7.

The OSI "Network" layer (3) matches the TCP/IP "Internet" layer, not the TCP/IP "Network Access" layer. The similar names confuse many students.

Chapter 1 Review

Chapter summary

  • Discrete quantities are counted (students, cars); continuous quantities are measured and can take any value (temperature, speed). Stairs vs ramp.
  • A digital system uses only two values, 0 (OFF/LOW/FALSE) and 1 (ON/HIGH/TRUE), called bits. It is reliable, accurate, easy to design and programmable.
  • Analog signals change smoothly (sine wave) and are affected by noise; digital signals jump between HIGH and LOW (square wave) and resist noise.
  • Boolean algebra, by George Boole, works with only TRUE (1) and FALSE (0) using variables, operations and expressions.
  • AND (·) gives 1 only when all inputs are 1; OR (+) gives 1 when any input is 1; NOT (') inverts its single input (unary).
  • A truth table lists all 2n input combinations in binary order with their outputs.
  • Logic gates are the building blocks of digital systems: AND, OR, NOT (basic); NAND, NOR (universal); XOR, XNOR (advanced).
  • NAND and NOR are universal: any circuit can be built using only one of them.
  • XOR gives 1 when inputs are different; XNOR gives 1 when inputs are the same.
  • Order of precedence: Parentheses → NOT → AND → OR. A logic diagram is drawn in the same order.
  • Minterms (SOP) come from rows with output 1; maxterms (POS) come from rows with output 0.
  • K-maps use Gray code (00, 01, 11, 10); group 1s in sizes 1, 2, 4, 8, as large as possible; overlap and wrap-around are allowed; changing variables are eliminated.
  • LogiSim Evolution simulates circuits: input pins are squares, output pins circles; the Poke Tool toggles inputs; bright green = 1, dark green = 0, blue = unconnected, red = error.
  • SDLC has 6 phases: Requirement Analysis (SRS), Design, Implementation, Testing (black box/white box), Deployment (direct/phased/pilot), Maintenance.
  • Waterfall is linear and suits fixed requirements (Al-Noor Library); Agile is iterative with 2–4 week sprints and suits changing requirements (Al-Noor Student App).
  • The OSI model (ISO, 1984) has 7 layers — "Please Do Not Throw Sausage Pizza Away"; the practical TCP/IP model has 4 layers: Application, Transport, Internet, Network Access.

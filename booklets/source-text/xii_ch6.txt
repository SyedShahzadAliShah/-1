##P126
6.1 Entrepreneur & Entrepreneurship
An entrepreneur is a person who identifies a problem, thinks of a solution, and
tries to change that solution into a product or service. An entrepreneur does not
always begin with a large company or a big office. Many begin with a small
idea, limited resources, and a strong wish to solve a problem.
They observe people and listen to their needs.
They take calculated risks.
They improve their idea step by step.
What is Entrepreneurship?
Entrepreneurship is the process of planning, starting, testing, and improving a
business idea. It involves creativity, problem-solving, teamwork, communication,
and calculated risk.
💡 Simple Meaning
An entrepreneur asks: What problem can I solve? Entrepreneurship is the
process of testing and improving that solution until it becomes useful for
people.

2. Solution

##P127
6.2 Entrepreneurship in the Digital Age
★ GOLDEN TOPIC
Entrepreneurship in the digital age means using digital tools to start, test,
improve, and grow a business idea. These tools may include mobile phones,
social media, websites, online forms, digital payments, maps, videos, and simple
apps.
The Power of Digital Tools
A digital entrepreneur does not always need to create a complex mobile app.
Sometimes a WhatsApp group, a Google Form, a Facebook page, or a simple
website is enough to test an idea.
In the past, many businesses needed large investments, offices, and long
planning. Today, a small idea can be tested quickly. If users do not like the idea,
the team can change it before spending too much money.
👨‍🏫 Real-World Relevance
A student selling handmade cards through Instagram or a shopkeeper
creating a WhatsApp catalogue for Ajrak are prime examples of modern
digital entrepreneurship without writing a single line of code!

##P129
6.3 From Problem to Business Idea
A good business idea usually starts from observing daily life. Students should
look around their home, college, market, village, or city and ask some simple
questions:
What problem do people face?
Who faces this problem?
How often does it happen?
What do people currently do to solve it?
Can technology help in a simple way?
Example Scenario
If students cannot find study material before exams, the problem is not only that
students are careless. The real issue may be that notes are shared in many places such
as WhatsApp, email, photocopy, and class groups. A possible digital idea can be a
simple subject-wise notes organizer where all files are placed in one location.
👨‍🏫 Scope of the Problem
Students should avoid choosing very large problems at the start (e.g.,
"improving the whole education system"). A better problem is smaller and
clear, such as helping Grade XII students track practical file submission
dates. Smaller problems are easier to test and improve.

##P131
6.4 Prototype
★ GOLDEN TOPIC
A prototype is an early model of a product, service, website, or app. It shows
how the idea may look or work. It is NOT the final product. It is made to test the
idea and collect feedback.
Why is a Prototype Useful?
A prototype helps students and entrepreneurs explain their idea clearly because
users can see something instead of only listening to words.
It makes the idea visible.
It helps the team find mistakes early.
Users can point out if a button is hard to find, if a screen has too much text, or
if a feature is missing.
It saves time and money before final coding begins.
👨‍🏫 What form can it take?
A prototype may be a paper sketch, a cardboard model, a clickable
PowerPoint, a Canva design, a Figma wireframe, a simple website layout, or
a sample service process chart. For example, for a canteen pre-order
system, students can just draw the screens on paper.
Page 8

##P132
6.5 Types of Prototypes
Prototypes are commonly divided into three types according to the level of
detail (fidelity). Fidelity means how close the prototype is to the final product.
6.5.1 Low-Fidelity Prototype
A quick and rough model made at the very beginning. Drawn on paper. Does not focus on
colors or fonts. Shows main idea and flow. Very suitable for students because it is cheap,
fast, and easy to change.
6.5.2 Mid-Fidelity Prototype
Clearer than a paper sketch. Created in PowerPoint, Canva, or Figma. Shows screens and
navigation in a more organized way, but without final graphics. Useful to test screen-to-
screen navigation.
6.5.3 High-Fidelity Prototype
Looks very close to the final product. Includes realistic colors, buttons, sample data, and
working features. Takes more time and should only be created after the basic idea is
tested.

##P134
6.6 Prototype Development Cycle
★ GOLDEN TOPIC
Prototype development is not a one-time activity. A prototype is created, tested,
improved, and tested again. This process is called iteration. The main stages
are design, build, test, and iterate.
1. Design
2. Build
3. Test
4. Iterate
1. Design Stage: Understand the problem and plan the solution. Decide what the
prototype should show (e.g., screen flow, features).
2. Build Stage: Create the first version (physical or digital). The aim is not beauty; the
aim is to make the idea visible.
3. Test Stage: Show the prototype to users. Observe what they understand and what
they find difficult.
4. Iterate Stage: Improve the prototype based on user feedback. Remove unnecessary
parts and add missing features.

6.8 Minimum Viable Product (MVP)
★ GOLDEN TOPIC
A Minimum Viable Product (MVP) is the simplest working version of a product. It
includes only the most important features needed to test the idea with real
users.
Minimum: It has only the essential features. No extras.
Viable: It is usable enough to actually solve the main problem.
An MVP is not the final product. It is also not only a drawing. It is a small working
version used to test if users actually need the solution.
Example: A complete canteen app may include login, payment, delivery tracking, and
discount offers. But the MVP may only be a simple Google Form where students select
snacks before break.
💡 Simple Meaning
An MVP answers: Will people actually use this solution? It tests real use and
behavior, not only the visual design.

6.10 Identifying the Riskiest Assumption
Every business idea is based on assumptions. An assumption is something we
believe to be true, but we have not tested it yet. The riskiest assumption is the
belief that can make the entire business fail if it turns out to be wrong.
Entrepreneurs should identify and test the riskiest assumption early because it
saves time and money.

6.11 Developing an MVP
To develop an MVP, the team must select only the essential features. Extra
features should be avoided at the beginning. A common mistake is trying to
build everything at once. This makes the product difficult, costly, and slow.
An MVP plan should clearly explain the problem, target users, main feature,
basic design, estimated cost, marketing method, feedback method, and
improvement plan.

6.12 Case Study: MVP for a Canteen
Problem: In a college, students wait in long lines at the canteen. Sometimes
snacks finish before students reach the counter. A group wants to test a
canteen pre-order idea.
Goal: Their aim is NOT to create a complete app immediately. Their aim is to test
whether students will place orders before break and whether the canteen can
manage these orders.
The Approach:
1. The team creates a paper prototype showing three steps: select item, enter name,
collect order.
2. Feedback from the prototype suggests the menu should be short and payment should
be at collection time.
3. For the actual MVP, the team uses a Google Form. Students select one of five items.
4. The canteen prepares these pre-orders. This MVP is tested for three days only to learn
quickly.
Table 6.9: MVP plan for canteen pre-order system
MVP Element
Decision
Features
Menu list, student name, class, item, and quantity.
Cost & Tools
Free Google Form or printed order slips.
Marketing
Class announcement and WhatsApp group message.
Testing period
Three days.
Feedback method
Ask students if they saved time and would use it again.
Improvement
Keep only 5 items; close orders 20 minutes before break.
Result: Students liked saving time, but canteen staff found too many items difficult. The
team improved the MVP by keeping only five items and creating a separate collection
line. This improvement is based on real feedback.

6.13 Testing an MVP
MVP testing means giving the basic working version to real users and studying
their response. The team should collect both numbers and comments.
Numbers show how many users tried the MVP and how many may use it
again.
Comments explain what users liked, disliked, or found confusing.
Testing should be planned before the MVP is launched. Decide how many users
will test it, how long the test will continue, and what information will be collected.

6.14 Beachhead Market
★ GOLDEN TOPIC
A beachhead market is a small and focused group of first users. These users
have a clear need and are likely to try the product early. Instead of trying to
serve everyone, entrepreneurs first focus on one small group. This helps them
test the MVP properly and improve it before expanding.
Characteristics of a Good Beachhead Market:
It should be specific and narrowly defined.
It should be reachable by the startup team.
The users should have a strong, immediate need for the solution.
For example, targeting "all students in Pakistan" is too broad for a new study app. "Grade
XII students preparing for board exams in one college" is much more focused and easier
to reach.

6.16 Ethical and Safe Use of Digital Tools
Digital entrepreneurship should be responsible. Students must respect privacy,
truthfulness, and fairness when collecting user feedback. They should not collect
unnecessary personal information or share phone numbers/photos without permission.
⚖️ Equity Point
A good digital solution should consider users who have weak internet,
limited devices, or language difficulty. A design with large buttons, simple
Urdu/Sindhi instructions, and low data usage helps ensure equal access.

Summary of Chapter 6
Entrepreneurship: The process of identifying a problem and testing/improving a
solution (product/service) until it becomes useful.
Digital Age: Entrepreneurs use accessible digital tools (social media, forms,
websites) to start and test ideas quickly with low investment.
Business Ideas: A good idea comes from observing daily life problems and
talking directly to users.
Prototype: An early model used to show and test the design of an idea. Can be
Low-Fidelity (paper), Mid-Fidelity (PowerPoint), or High-Fidelity (realistic code).
Development Cycle: Consists of Design, Build, Test, and Iterate. Feedback is
crucial for improvement.
MVP (Minimum Viable Product): The simplest working version of a product used
to test real market demand and user behavior.
Prototype vs MVP: Prototype tests "Does it look right?", MVP tests "Will people
actually use it?".
Riskiest Assumption: The core belief that, if wrong, will cause the business to fail.
Must be tested early.
Beachhead Market: A small, focused group of first users with a strong need for
the product. Easier to reach and learn from than a broad market.
Ethics & Equity: Digital solutions must respect user privacy, be truthful, and
consider accessibility for users with limited resources or language barriers.

##P113
6.1 Introduction to Digital Literacy
Digital literacy refers to the ability to understand and use digital devices, the
internet, and online tools in a safe, effective, and responsible manner. In our
increasingly digital world, possessing digital literacy is essential for everyone.
It enables individuals to find information, communicate, produce digital content,
and solve problems through technology.
💡 Did you know?
Digital literacy is not only using devices; it is using digital information safely
and wisely. A digitally literate student can locate information, organize it
properly, turn it into something useful, and share it responsibly.
6.2 Digital Literacy in the Information Age
The Information Age is a period where vast amounts of data are produced,
shared, and accessed instantly through the internet, mobile devices, and digital
platforms.
Simply having access to technology is not enough.
Students and users must develop the skills to understand, manage, and use
information wisely.
Digital literacy goes beyond basic computer skills; it involves technical,
cognitive, and social abilities to function successfully in a digital society.

6.4 Data-Collection Strategies
★ GOLDEN TOPIC
Digital literacy is not limited to finding information that already exists. Students
should also learn how to gather original information. Below are the common
strategies used to collect data.
Interviews
Surveys
Prototypes
Observation
Simulations
6.4.1 Interviews
An interview is a planned conversation used to gather information directly from a
person. It may be one-to-one or in a small group. Questions are planned in advance,
and answers are usually qualitative.
6.4.2 Surveys / Questionnaires
A survey is useful when the same set of questions needs to be asked to many people.
It may be on paper or created with digital tools like Google Forms or Microsoft Forms.
Note: Online survey tools automatically collect responses and display basic charts,
making data analysis much easier.

##P116
Data-Collection Strategies (Continued)
6.4.3 Prototypes
A prototype is a simple early version of a product, model, or solution. It allows an idea
to be tested before time and effort are spent on the final version. Users try the
prototype and give feedback, which becomes useful data for improvement.
6.4.4 Observation
In observation, the researcher watches what people do or what happens in a situation
and records the results carefully. No direct questions are asked. The data may be
qualitative (notes) or quantitative (counts).
6.4.5 Simulations
A simulation is a computer-based model of a real situation. Variables can be
changed to see what happens. Simulations are highly useful when real experiments
are costly, risky, or difficult to perform.
👨‍🏫 Teacher's Tip
Ask students which method they would use to find out "how many cars cross
an intersection at 8 AM" vs "how students feel about the new cafeteria food".
(Observation vs. Survey/Interview).

6.5 Primary and Secondary Data
★ GOLDEN TOPIC
Whenever data is used, it is important to know where it came from. The source
affects how much control the researcher had over collecting it and how
confidently the results can be used.
6.5.1 Primary Data
Primary data is collected first-hand
by the researcher for a particular
purpose. It is gathered directly from
people, events, or situations rather
than taken from an already published
source.
Sources: Interviews, surveys,
observations, experiments.
6.5.2 Secondary Data
Secondary data has already been
collected by someone else and is
being used again for a new purpose. It
is extracted from existing archives.
Sources: Books, research articles,
official reports, web statistics, online
databases.

6.6 Designing a Data-Collection Approach
Before collecting data, a researcher must have a plan. This ensures accurate,
relevant, and useful information is gathered.
1. Define the research question clearly.
2. Decide the type of data needed: quantitative, qualitative, or both.
3. Select suitable methods (survey, interview, observation).
4. Choose the sample or target group.
5. Prepare the tools such as forms, questions, and observation sheets.
6. Collect the data carefully and accurately.
7. Store the data digitally in a spreadsheet and keep a backup.
8. Follow ethical practice and respect privacy (Consent is mandatory).

6.7 Presenting Data Using Digital Tools
Collecting data is only one part of the work. Students must also present their
findings so others can understand them quickly and easily. Digital tools make
this much easier.
6.7.1 Spreadsheets
Spreadsheets such as MS Excel, Google Sheets, and Apple Numbers help students
arrange information in rows and columns.
Enter data systematically.
Calculate totals, averages, and percentages.
Sort and filter records.
Create charts and graphs automatically.
6.7.2 Presentations
Software like PowerPoint, Google Slides, and Canva helps students explain findings to
an audience in an organized way.
Begin with a clear title and research question.
Use short bullet points instead of long paragraphs.
Include tables, charts, and key findings.
Keep the design simple and readable.

##P120
Presenting Data (Continued)
6.7.3 Infographics
An infographic is a visual way of presenting information, facts, or data so that people
can understand the main idea quickly and easily. Tools such as Canva, Power BI, or
Tableau can be used.
Simplify complex concepts.
Highlight the most important findings and results.
Use clear visuals, limited text, and accurate information.
6.7.4 Reports
Reports are structured documents used to present collected data, findings, analysis,
and conclusions. Tools like MS Word or Google Docs are standard.
Start with a clear title, introduction, and purpose.
Present the collected data using tables or charts.
Explain findings in simple language.
Include conclusions and recommendations based on the data.
Optional: Use AI tools carefully to improve wording, but always review for accuracy.
👨‍🏫 Visual Rule of Thumb
A good chart or infographic can explain complex data faster than a long
paragraph. However, visuals must always be accurate and based strictly on
the real data collected, never exaggerated.

Students first search for background information using search engines. They use

Source Evaluation Checklist:
Is the author/organization clearly mentioned?
Is the information recent?
Is the website reliable (.edu, .gov, research-based)?
Does the source provide facts/references, or is it biased?
Step 2: Identify Data Types
Students must decide what kind of primary data they will collect.
Quantitative: Number of hours used daily, percentage who feel distracted.
Qualitative: Opinions on why they get distracted, suggestions for better use.
Step 3: Select Data-Collection Methods
Since they need to ask the same questions to many students, a Survey/Questionnaire
(via Google Forms or paper) is the most efficient method.

Step 6: Collect Primary Data (Ethically)
When conducting the survey, students must follow strict ethical guidelines:
Ask permission before collecting responses (Consent).
Avoid collecting sensitive personal information.
Do not record names unless absolutely necessary (Anonymity).
Keep the responses private.
Use the data only for educational purposes.

##P125
Analysis and Conclusion (Steps 10-12)
Step 10: Analyze the Results
By looking at the spreadsheet and charts, the group identifies trends:
Most students (majority) use phones for 3-5 hours daily.
Half use it for educational purposes (lectures/notes).
A clear trend: Students using phones mostly for entertainment report much higher
distraction levels than those using them for study.
Step 11: Write the Conclusion
"The research shows mobile phones have both positive and negative effects. They
provide access to learning materials. However, excessive use of social media and
gaming reduces concentration. Therefore, phones should not be banned, but
managed wisely."
Step 12: Recommendations
Based on the data, the students recommend:
Turn off social media notifications while studying.
Set daily screen-time limits.
Take physical breaks from screens to protect health.

Careers & Summary
Career Opportunities in Digital Research
Market Research Analyst: Designs surveys to collect primary data to guide business
decisions.
UX (User Experience) Researcher: Gathers qualitative/quantitative data to improve app
and website designs.
Data Journalist: Uses advanced search skills to research stories and presents them via
infographics.
Digital Marketing Specialist: Collects audience data and presents results through
dashboards and reports.
Summary
Digital Literacy: Not just using devices, but finding, evaluating, and creating information
safely and responsibly.
Data Types: Qualitative (opinions, words) vs. Quantitative (numbers, counts).
Data Sources: Primary (collected first-hand by you) vs. Secondary (already collected by
someone else).
Collection Methods: Interviews, Surveys, Prototypes, Observation, and Simulations.
Advanced Search: Using operators like quotes (" ") or filetype:pdf to find reliable sources
efficiently.
Ethics: Always ask for permission (consent) and respect user privacy when collecting data.
Digital Tools: Spreadsheets (organize/calculate), Presentations (deliver to audience),
Infographics (quick visual summaries), Reports (detailed structure).
Digital Artefact: The final product that effectively communicates the answer to the
research question using evidence and visuals.
